# Middle East Overview

## Scope
This section covers the religious traditions of the ancient Near East, ancient Israel and the Levant, Judaism, early Christianity, Islam, and the wider late antique and medieval worlds in which these traditions developed, competed, and reshaped one another — from Bronze Age Mesopotamian and Levantine temple systems through the Second Temple and rabbinic periods, the Christianizing Roman and Sasanian worlds, and Islam's emergence and expansion, into the medieval and early modern periods this shelf's individual notes carry the story forward into (Sufism, Shia Islam, Islamic philosophy).

## Why the Middle East matters religiously
This shelf is one of the library's major load-bearing walls.
The Middle East is central to the history of:
- temple religion and divine kingship
- prophecy and covenant
- scriptural monotheism
- apocalyptic imagination
- Christianity's formation
- Islam's revelation and civilizational expansion
- late antique sectarian diversity and intense competition over truth, text, law, and empire

If Africa is under-read and Europe is over-centered, the Middle East is often simplified into just "the Abrahamic zone" — three religions treated as a closed, self-contained monotheistic family. That is too narrow in two directions at once. First, its earlier polytheistic and imperial religious layers (Mesopotamian, Canaanite, Zoroastrian) are not mere prologue; they shape the vocabulary, imagery, and institutional forms that "Abrahamic" monotheism later works with and against — see [[Ancient Near Eastern Religions]] and [[Zoroastrianism]]. Second, treating Judaism, Christianity, and Islam as one settled family obscures how much internal diversity, contest, and historical rupture each one actually contains — none of the three arrived at its familiar modern form in one step, and this shelf's individual notes try to hold that complexity rather than smoothing it into a single monotheistic throughline.

## Major historical layers
### Ancient Near Eastern religions
Mesopotamian, Levantine, and related temple systems with divine councils, sacrifice, kingship, omen-reading, and imperial theology — see [[Ancient Near Eastern Religions]].

### Ancient Israel and Judaism
Covenant, law, prophecy, temple, exile, textual formation, and rabbinic reinterpretation after major historical ruptures — including a pre-exilic religious world that was itself substantially polytheistic before the emergence of strict monotheism, a divergence between the Hebrew Bible's own retrospective account and what historians and archaeologists can independently establish that is worth taking seriously rather than smoothing over. See [[Ancient Israelite Religion]] and [[Judaism Overview]].

### Zoroastrianism and the Persian world
Persian Zoroastrianism — with its own prophetic figure, cosmic dualism, and eschatology — is not peripheral to the "Abrahamic" story; Persian imperial patronage and cultural contact (particularly during and after the Achaemenid period) is widely thought by scholars to have influenced later Jewish angelology, eschatology, and dualistic imagery, making it a live thread running through the rest of this shelf rather than an isolated case. See [[Zoroastrianism]].

### Early Christianity in the region
A Jewish-origin movement that develops in the eastern Mediterranean and Near East, shaped by Greek and Syriac language worlds, councils, empire, monasticism, and genuine early theological contest before anything like a single orthodoxy existed to define deviation against. See [[Early Christianity in the Middle East]] and [[Syriac Christianity]].

### Islam
Prophetic revelation, scripture, law, ritual life, communal order, empire, theology, and later mystical, legal, and sectarian complexity — including the Sunni–Shia divide over succession and authority and the rich internal jurisprudential debate (sighting vs. calculation of the lunar calendar, the legitimacy of Mawlid observance) that shows Islamic practice as an actively argued tradition rather than a fixed, uniform one. See [[Islam Overview]], [[Islam Ritual Calendar]], [[Shia Islam]], [[Sufism]], and [[Islamic Philosophy]].

### Late antique and sectarian diversity
Manichaean and Gnostic-type currents, apocalyptic movements, heresies, mystics, and competing communities all matter for understanding the region's religious ecology — a genuinely crowded, contested field, not a clean two-or-three-religion map. See [[Manichaeism]].

## Core themes worth tracking
- prophecy, revelation, and scripture
- law, covenant, and communal order
- temple, sacrifice, and post-temple transformation
- monotheism emerging gradually and contestedly from polytheistic and imperial worlds, not appearing fully formed
- late antique competition over truth and authority
- religion and empire as mutually shaping forces, from Achaemenid Persia through Rome, the caliphates, and beyond
- sectarian fragmentation and canon formation as recurring, comparable processes across Judaism, Christianity, and Islam alike

## Research lenses that help here
- do not treat monotheism as if it appears in a vacuum, or as if it arrived complete rather than developing across centuries of contest with polytheistic and henotheistic alternatives
- keep temple, prophecy, empire, and law in view at the same time
- distinguish a tradition's own retrospective self-account (emic) from what historians, archaeologists, and textual critics can independently establish (etic) — and say plainly where the two diverge, as they do for pre-exilic Israelite religion, the "parting of the ways" between Judaism and Christianity, and the early history of Sunni-Shia division
- distinguish scripture from later interpretive institutions
- watch how theology and political sovereignty reinforce one another, from Achaemenid and Sasanian Persia through Rome and the caliphates
- remember that late antiquity is crowded, not clean — Zoroastrian, Manichaean, Gnostic, and various Jewish and Christian sectarian currents all compete in the same space rather than the field narrowing neatly to three "Abrahamic" religions

## Good working summary
The Middle East is one of the most historically dense religious zones in the world: temple systems, prophecy, scripture, empire, law, monotheism, sectarian struggle, and late antique transformation all converge here — and each tradition on this shelf carries real, still-studied internal diversity rather than settling into one uniform "Abrahamic" story.
