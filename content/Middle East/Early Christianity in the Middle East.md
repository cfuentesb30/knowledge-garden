# Early Christianity in the Middle East

## Scope
This note focuses on Christianity as it first develops in the eastern Mediterranean and Near Eastern world before becoming a predominantly European story in common summaries.

## Why it matters
Christianity begins as a Jewish movement in the Roman Near East, not as an originally European religion.
The Middle Eastern setting matters for its language, scripture, disputes, liturgy, monasticism, councils, and institutional spread.
A Europe-only reading badly distorts the story.

## Foundational dates and phases
- **1st century CE** — Jesus movement emerges in Roman Judea
- **c. 30 CE** — death of Jesus
- **1st century CE** — apostolic communities spread through the eastern Mediterranean
- **70 CE** — Roman destruction of the Jerusalem Temple
- **132–135 CE** — Bar Kokhba revolt
- **c. 144 CE** — Marcion excommunicated at Rome
- **270–271 CE onward** — Anthony of Egypt withdraws into the desert; organized Christian monasticism begins
- **313 CE** — Edict of Milan legalizes Christianity in the Roman Empire
- **325 CE** — Council of Nicaea
- **431 CE** — Council of Ephesus; Church of the East separates
- **451 CE** — Council of Chalcedon, major Christological divide point producing the Oriental Orthodox communion

## Jewish roots
Christianity begins inside a Jewish world shaped by:
- scripture
- prophecy
- temple memory
- law and interpretation
- messianic expectation
- sectarian diversity — see [[Judaism Overview]] on Second Temple Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, and other currents; the Jesus movement is best understood as one apocalyptic Jewish movement among several rather than a wholly separate religion from its outset

Without that background, Christianity becomes unintelligible. Two events reshape the relationship between the Jesus movement and the wider Jewish world it emerged from: the Roman destruction of the Jerusalem Temple in **70 CE**, which removed the sacrificial-priestly institution both non-Christian and Christian Jews had shared, and the **Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135 CE)**, after which messianic claims tied to armed revolt became politically dangerous in a way that likely widened the practical distance between rabbinic Judaism and a Jesus movement increasingly composed of non-Jews.

### The "parting of the ways" — a contested timeline
Older scholarship (associated especially with James D. G. Dunn's *The Partings of the Ways*) described Judaism and Christianity as separating into two distinct religions relatively early, essentially complete by the end of the 1st or early 2nd century. More recent scholarship — Daniel Boyarin's work is the most influential challenge — argues the boundary stayed genuinely blurred for centuries longer: Jewish and Christian communities in some regions continued to overlap, share practices, and contest identity with each other well into late antiquity, and a firm institutional separation only became fully enforceable once Christianity held state power from the 4th century onward. This is a live, unresolved debate about timeline and mechanism, not a disagreement about whether Christianity has Jewish origins (no serious scholar disputes that).

## Core beliefs taking shape
- Jesus as Messiah / Christ
- resurrection
- salvation and redemption
- relation between Jesus' humanity and divinity
- scriptural continuity/discontinuity with Judaism
- sacrament, church authority, and orthodoxy formation

### Diversity before orthodoxy
Before anything like a single "orthodox" position existed to define deviation against, the 2nd century was a genuinely open contest over what following Jesus meant. Two instructive poles:
- **Ebionites** — a Jewish-Christian current that maintained Torah observance (circumcision, kosher law, Sabbath), regarded Jesus as a human Messiah rather than a pre-existent divine being, and rejected Paul's letters as a corruption of authentic Jewish practice
- **Marcion of Sinope** (excommunicated at Rome, c. 144 CE) — took the opposite extreme, teaching that the creator God of Israelite scripture was a lesser, flawed deity distinct from the higher God revealed by Jesus, and compiling what may be the earliest attested Christian scriptural canon: a shortened Gospel of Luke plus ten Pauline letters, with the Jewish scriptures excluded entirely

Both were eventually rejected as the emerging mainstream consolidated, but their existence — alongside various currents later lumped together as "gnostic" (see [[../Europe/Gnosticism in Context|Gnosticism in Context]] and [[../Europe/Nag Hammadi and Gnostic Texts|Nag Hammadi and Gnostic Texts]]) — shows that questions later settled by council (Jesus' relation to the Jewish scriptures, his divinity, which texts counted as authoritative) were live and contested for generations, not derived in a straight line from a single original teaching.

## Ritual life
Early and late antique Christianity develops:
- baptism
- Eucharist
- liturgical prayer
- fasting cycles
- saint and martyr veneration
- monastic discipline
- pilgrimage and sacred-site devotion

## Language worlds
Christianity in the Middle East developed in:
- Aramaic / Syriac — see [[Syriac Christianity]] for the full treatment of this language world and the communities it produced, including the earliest known gospel harmony, Tatian's **Diatessaron** (composed c. 170 CE, likely in Syriac or Greek, weaving all four gospels into one continuous narrative), which remained the primary gospel text in some Syriac-speaking churches for centuries before separate-gospel texts displaced it
- Greek, the dominant language of the eastern Mediterranean intellectual and administrative world, and the language of the New Testament documents themselves
- Coptic (Egypt and adjacent worlds), the language of Egyptian Christianity and of much surviving early monastic and Gnostic literature (including the Nag Hammadi codices — see [[../Europe/Nag Hammadi and Gnostic Texts|Nag Hammadi and Gnostic Texts]])
- later Arabic in many contexts, as the region came under Islamic rule from the 7th century onward — see [[Islam Overview]]

Language matters because theology, liturgy, and transmission change with language world: a Syriac theological vocabulary built around Semitic idiom (the divine "womb," the Spirit as maternal) does not map cleanly onto Greek philosophical categories, and disputes that read as purely doctrinal in Greek sometimes involved real translation and conceptual mismatch across language communities — a factor some historians argue was underappreciated in how quickly christological disputes hardened into permanent schisms.

## Important communities and traditions
Two rival intellectual centers anchor much of the region's early theological development:
- **Antioch**, in Syria, is where the book of Acts (11:26) records the disciples were "first called Christians," and it became the seat of an influential theological school and patriarchate associated with more literal, historically-grounded scriptural interpretation and a Christology emphasizing the distinctness of Christ's human and divine natures
- **Alexandria**, in Egypt, developed its own Catechetical School — under **Clement of Alexandria** (c. 150–215) and then **Origen** (c. 185–254), who became head of the school around 203 — associated with allegorical scriptural interpretation, engagement with Greek philosophy, and a Christology emphasizing the unity of Christ's divine and human nature; Origen in particular is one of the most important and controversial early Christian theologians, later condemned (posthumously, at the Second Council of Constantinople in 553) for some of his more speculative positions even as his exegetical and theological method shaped centuries of subsequent thought

These two centers' differing theological instincts feed directly into the christological controversies below. For the fuller regional and linguistic communities that these controversies eventually produced or hardened — Syriac Orthodox, Church of the East, Coptic, Armenian — see [[Syriac Christianity]], which covers their post-schism institutional history, geographic spread, and major figures in depth; this note focuses on the shared formative period before and through that fragmentation.

## Councils and conflict
Councils matter because they are not just meetings.
They are mechanisms for defining orthodoxy, excluding rivals, and stabilizing doctrine, called and enforced by emperors as much as by bishops.
That means Christology, canon, liturgy, and imperial politics are intertwined.

### Nicaea (325 CE) and the Arian controversy
The Alexandrian presbyter **Arius** taught that the Son (the Logos/Christ) was not co-eternal with God the Father but was brought into being by the Father — "there was [a time] when he was not," in the phrase attributed to Arius's opponents' summary of his position. **Athanasius**, then a young deacon and later bishop of Alexandria, became Arius's most persistent opponent. The **Emperor Constantine** convened the Council of Nicaea in 325 CE — the first council intended to represent the whole church — largely to resolve this dispute and secure doctrinal unity across his newly favored religion. The resulting Nicene Creed declared the Son *homoousios* ("of one substance") with the Father, condemning Arius's position, though the controversy did not actually end there: Arian and various compromise positions remained politically and theologically powerful for decades afterward, and the dispute was not considered fully settled by the wider church until the First Council of Constantinople in 381.

### Chalcedon (451 CE) and lasting fracture
The **Council of Chalcedon** addressed a different but related question: how to describe the relationship between Christ's divine and human natures. Chalcedon's formula — one person in two natures, "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation" — was intended as a mediating position, but it satisfied neither extreme. Those who held that Christ has a single, unified nature (a position often labeled "Miaphysite" by supporters and "Monophysite," a somewhat pejorative simplification, by opponents) rejected Chalcedon and became what is now called the Oriental Orthodox communion — Syriac Orthodox, Coptic, Armenian, and Ethiopian churches. This fracture — alongside the earlier separation of the Church of the East after the Council of Ephesus in 431 over the Nestorian controversy — produced the lasting, still-existing theological map of Middle Eastern and Eastern Christianity described in full in [[Syriac Christianity]].

## Monastic and institutional significance
The Middle East is also central for:
- early monasticism, beginning with **Anthony of Egypt** (c. 251–356), who withdrew into the Egyptian desert around 270–271 CE and became the model for solitary, hermitic asceticism (*anchoritic* monasticism) — his biography, written by Athanasius of Alexandria, was hugely influential in spreading the monastic ideal across the wider Christian world
- **Pachomius** (c. 292–348), also in Egypt, who organized communal (*cenobitic*) monasteries — shared living under a common rule — as an alternative to Anthony's solitary model, founding a federation of monastic communities that became the template for later Christian monastic institutions generally
- ascetic discipline more broadly, understood by practitioners as a form of interior martyrdom once outright Roman persecution ended after 313 CE — a continuity of purpose (bodily discipline as spiritual struggle) worth reading alongside [[../Cross-Regional/Martyrdom, Suffering, and Redemptive Pain|Martyrdom, Suffering, and Redemptive Pain]]
- theological schooling, principally at Alexandria and Antioch, described above
- saint cults and relic devotion — see [[../Cross-Regional/Relics, Icons, and Sacred Objects|Relics, Icons, and Sacred Objects]]
- pilgrimage networks converging on Jerusalem and other sacred sites, especially following the Roman state's official favor after Constantine — see [[../Cross-Regional/Sacred Geography and Pilgrimage|Sacred Geography and Pilgrimage]]

These are not late decorations. They are part of Christianity's formation, and Egypt and Syria specifically — not Rome — are where organized Christian monasticism as an institution begins, spreading westward into Europe only afterward. See also [[../Cross-Regional/Priests, Monks, Shamans, and Ritual Specialists|Priests, Monks, Shamans, and Ritual Specialists]].

## Why it matters comparatively
Early Christianity in the Middle East is crucial for studying:
- continuity and rupture with Judaism, and the contested timeline of their separation
- scripture and canon formation amid real doctrinal diversity, not a single agreed starting point
- orthodoxy and heresy as categories produced by conflict and council, not given in advance
- councils and imperial theology, and how doctrinal and political power reinforce each other
- liturgy and sacred language, and how theology shifts with the language it's argued in
- monasticism and saint veneration as Egyptian and Syrian institutional innovations, not later Western additions

## Good working summary
Christianity begins and matures in a Middle Eastern environment marked by Jewish roots, genuine early doctrinal diversity, Greek and Syriac thought, imperial politics, liturgy, language diversity, and council-driven conflict. If you strip out that regional depth — or flatten the diversity into a single unbroken line from Jesus to Nicene orthodoxy — you get a sanitized and misleading story.
