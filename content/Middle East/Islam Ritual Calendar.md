# Islam Ritual Calendar

## Purpose
This note maps the major ritual times and observances of the Islamic calendar in more depth, as a companion to [[Islam Overview]] (the fuller doctrinal and historical treatment) and [[Sufism]] (for the devotional practices — shrine visitation, *'urs* death-anniversary celebrations — that intersect with several dates below).

## Calendar basis
Islam uses a purely **lunar** calendar (the Hijri calendar), dated from the Hijra — Muhammad's migration from Mecca to Medina in 622 CE — with twelve months of 29 or 30 days determined by the lunar cycle, totaling roughly 354 days a year, about 11 days shorter than the solar year. Unlike the Jewish or Chinese calendars, the Islamic calendar has no periodic intercalation (leap month) to keep it aligned with the seasons — a deliberate choice with scriptural grounding (Qur'an 9:36-37 is traditionally read as prohibiting intercalation). This means every fixed Islamic observance — Ramadan, Hajj, all of it — cycles slowly backward through the solar year, completing a full cycle through all four seasons roughly every 33 years. A Ramadan observed in midwinter and one observed in midsummer are separated by about 16–17 years, not by anything irregular.

### The twelve months
In order: **Muharram**, **Safar**, **Rabi al-Awwal**, **Rabi al-Thani**, **Jumada al-Ula**, **Jumada al-Akhirah**, **Rajab**, **Sha'ban**, **Ramadan**, **Shawwal**, **Dhu al-Qi'dah**, **Dhu al-Hijjah**. Four of these (Muharram, Rajab, Dhu al-Qi'dah, and Dhu al-Hijjah) are traditionally designated **sacred months** (*al-ashhur al-hurum*) in which warfare was historically prohibited or restricted in pre-Islamic and early Islamic Arabian custom, a status the Qur'an affirms (9:36).

### How months begin: sighting versus calculation
A live and unresolved practical question across the Muslim world is how a new lunar month is determined. The classical majority juristic position, grounded in hadith instructing believers to fast and break the fast based on **sighting the crescent moon** (*ru'yat al-hilal*), holds that actual observational sighting — not astronomical prediction — should determine a month's start, since worship obligations should rest on certain, observed fact rather than probabilistic calculation. Since the 20th century, however, some Muslim-majority states and organizations (Turkey and Indonesia's Muhammadiyah movement among the more prominent examples) have adopted **astronomical calculation** (*hisab*) instead, for consistency and predictability. The practical result is that Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr, and Eid al-Adha do not always fall on the same day worldwide — a visible, recurring instance of unresolved internal juristic disagreement rather than a settled matter with one community simply "doing it wrong."

## Major observances

### Ramadan
The ninth month; the month of obligatory fasting (*sawm*, one of the Five Pillars — see [[Islam Overview]]) from dawn to sunset, involving abstention from food, drink, and other specified acts, alongside increased prayer, Qur'an recitation (the month is traditionally associated with the revelation of the Qur'an — see Laylat al-Qadr below), and charity. Ramadan observance is nightly as well as daily: **tarawih**, extended voluntary night prayers, are widely performed in congregation, and the pre-dawn meal (*suhur*) and sunset meal breaking the fast (*iftar*) structure daily life for the month.

### Laylat al-Qadr ("Night of Power")
Commemorated within the last ten nights of Ramadan (most commonly observed on the 27th night, though the Qur'an and hadith leave the exact night deliberately unspecified, and different schools and communities favor different odd-numbered nights), this marks the night tradition holds the Qur'an's revelation to Muhammad began. The Qur'an itself (Surah 97) describes it as "better than a thousand months" — making it, by traditional reckoning, the single most spiritually significant night of the Islamic year.

### Eid al-Fitr
"Festival of the Breaking of the Fast," on the first of Shawwal, immediately following Ramadan; marked by communal prayer, charity specifically obligatory before the prayer (*zakat al-fitr*), new clothing, and family gathering. One of the two major festivals in Islam.

### Dhu al-Hijjah and the Hajj
The twelfth month, centered on the **Hajj**, the pilgrimage to Mecca (the fifth Pillar, obligatory once in a lifetime for those physically and financially able — see [[../Cross-Regional/Sacred Geography and Pilgrimage|Sacred Geography and Pilgrimage]]). The Hajj follows a fixed multi-day ritual sequence in the early part of the month:
- pilgrims enter **ihram** (a consecrated state marked by specific dress and behavioral restrictions) and perform **tawaf** (sevenfold counter-clockwise circumambulation of the Kaaba) and **sa'i** (walking between the hills of Safa and Marwa, commemorating Hagar's search for water)
- the **standing at Arafat** (9th Dhu al-Hijjah, *Yawm Arafah*) — widely described in Islamic tradition as the ritual and spiritual center of the entire Hajj; a pilgrimage without this standing is not considered valid
- **Ramy al-Jamarat**, the ritual stoning of three pillars at Mina, commemorating Ibrahim's (Abraham's) rejection of Satan's temptation not to sacrifice his son — connecting the Hajj directly to the Ibrahim/Ismail narrative that also underlies Eid al-Adha
- a sacrifice (commemorating the same narrative — see below), followed by the cutting or shaving of hair and the exit from ihram

### Eid al-Adha
"Festival of the Sacrifice," on the 10th of Dhu al-Hijjah, coinciding with and marking the close of the main Hajj rites; commemorates Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice his son at God's command before God provided a ram in substitution. (Islamic tradition identifies the son in this narrative as **Ismail**, in contrast to the Genesis account's Isaac — a divergence in a shared Abrahamic narrative that is worth naming explicitly rather than glossing over, since it reflects a real point of distinct scriptural tradition rather than a minor variant.) Observed worldwide, including by Muslims not performing the Hajj that year, through communal prayer and the ritual sacrifice of an animal, with the meat traditionally divided between family, relatives/friends, and the poor. Along with Eid al-Fitr, it is one of the two major festivals of the Islamic calendar.

### Ashura (10th of Muharram)
One of the clearest cases in the Islamic ritual calendar of a single date carrying entirely different weight and meaning across the Sunni–Shia divide:
- In **Sunni** practice, Ashura is a recommended (not obligatory) fast day, understood as commemorating God's deliverance of Moses and the Israelites from Pharaoh — a tradition with reported roots in Muhammad's own practice of fasting on this day, partly in continuity with Jewish observance of Yom Kippur in Medina.
- In **Shia** practice, Ashura marks the anniversary of the **Battle of Karbala** (680 CE), in which **Husayn ibn Ali**, Muhammad's grandson, and a small group of family and supporters were killed by Umayyad forces after refusing to pledge allegiance to the caliph Yazid. This is a foundational event in Shia religious identity (see [[Shia Islam]]) commemorated with mourning processions, passion-play reenactments (*ta'ziyeh*), and in some communities ritual self-flagellation — practices that are themselves debated and contested within Shia communities, not universally practiced or endorsed. **Arba'een**, forty days after Ashura, marks the end of the mourning period and draws one of the largest annual pilgrim gatherings in the world to Karbala, Iraq.

### Mawlid al-Nabi (12th of Rabi al-Awwal, by the most common — though not universal — dating)
Commemorates the birth of Muhammad. This is one of the more genuinely contested observances within Islam rather than a settled universal practice: it is widely celebrated across much of the Sunni and Shia world (with public celebration, recitation of devotional poetry narrating Muhammad's life, and communal gatherings), but Salafi and Wahhabi currents reject it as *bid'a* (unwarranted religious innovation), on the grounds that it was not practiced by Muhammad, his companions, or the earliest generations, and object in particular to practices some celebrations include (standing in reverence, use of musical instruments). Mawlid is not observed as an official holiday in Saudi Arabia. This dispute is a live, unresolved one inside Islamic legal and theological discourse, not a fringe disagreement.

### Isra' and Mi'raj (27th of Rajab, by common though not universal dating)
Commemorates the Night Journey (*Isra'*) from Mecca to Jerusalem and the subsequent Ascension (*Mi'raj*) through the heavens, during which tradition holds the obligation of the five daily prayers was established. Observed with additional prayer and recitation; like several dates on this calendar, its exact night is a matter of some variation across sources and communities rather than a single textually fixed date.

### Laylat al-Bara'at (mid-Sha'ban, the night of the 15th)
A night associated in some traditions (more prominently in South Asian and some Sufi-influenced devotional currents than universally across the Muslim world) with the recording or determination of the coming year's fates and with seeking forgiveness; its observance and theological significance vary considerably by region and school, and it carries less universal weight than Laylat al-Qadr.

### Friday prayer (Jumu'ah)
Weekly rather than annual, but structurally central: the main congregational prayer, replacing the noon prayer on Fridays, including a sermon (*khutbah*) — the primary regular setting in which Islamic communities receive public religious instruction and, historically, political/communal announcements. Attendance at Friday congregational prayer is treated as obligatory for adult Muslim men in classical jurisprudence, with varying positions across schools and communities on women's attendance.

## Why it matters
Islamic ritual time is not incidental scenery around belief — it is one of the primary structures through which Islamic law, communal memory, and daily/annual discipline actually operate, and the calendar's genuine internal contested points (sighting vs. calculation, Mawlid's legitimacy, how Ashura is marked) are as much a part of understanding lived Islam as the observances themselves are.
