# Judaism Overview

## Scope
Judaism is a covenantal religious tradition centered on God, Torah, law, memory, peoplehood, interpretation, ritual life, and historical continuity.

## Why it matters
Judaism matters not only because it is historically prior to Christianity and Islam in some key respects, but because it is a major religious civilization in its own right.
It is essential for understanding:
- covenant and law
- scripture and interpretation
- temple and post-temple transformation
- diaspora identity
- monotheism as lived communal order
- memory, exile, return, and historical self-understanding

## Historical frame
### Ancient Israelite and biblical roots
Judaism emerges from the world of ancient Israel and Judah, temple worship, covenantal identity, prophetic critique, monarchy, exile, and text formation. The pre-exilic religious landscape this grows out of was itself substantially polytheistic and only gradually, contestedly, becomes strict monotheism — see [[Ancient Israelite Religion]] for the fuller etic, historical-critical account of that development, which the biblical text itself presents very differently (as a single unbroken revealed monotheism from the outset) than the archaeological and textual-critical record supports. That divergence between the tradition's own retrospective account and what historians can independently establish is worth stating plainly rather than collapsing into either the text's own narrative or a purely skeptical dismissal of it.

### Second Temple period (c. 516 BCE – 70 CE)
This period is crucial for:
- temple centrality, as the rebuilt Jerusalem Temple (following the return from Babylonian exile) remained the focal point of sacrificial worship and priestly authority
- **sectarian diversity**: rather than one normative Judaism, this period included multiple, actively competing groups. The **Pharisees** emphasized both written Torah and an oral interpretive tradition, believed in bodily resurrection, and are widely regarded (though the lines of direct institutional continuity are debated) as the closest forerunners of later rabbinic Judaism. The **Sadducees**, drawn largely from priestly and aristocratic circles, accepted only the written Torah as authoritative, rejected doctrines like resurrection that they saw as unscriptural, and were closely tied to Temple administration — meaning their influence effectively ended with the Temple's destruction. The **Essenes**, known largely through the **Dead Sea Scrolls** discovered at Qumran from 1947 onward, practiced a stricter, often communal and ascetic form of Jewish life, apocalyptic expectation, and — in some reconstructions — celibacy, though the precise identification of the Qumran community with "the Essenes" described by Josephus and Philo is itself debated among specialists rather than fully settled. Other currents, including Zealot-associated revolutionary movements and various apocalyptic groups, complicate the picture further. Before the Dead Sea Scrolls' discovery, this diversity was far less visible to scholars, who had largely reconstructed Second Temple Judaism through later rabbinic and Christian sources alone
- scriptural consolidation, as the boundaries of what would become the Hebrew Bible's canon developed (see [[../Cross-Regional/Scripture Canon and Authority|Scripture Canon and Authority]])
- legal and interpretive development that lays groundwork for later rabbinic method
- the world in which early Christianity later emerges as one further messianic and apocalyptic current among these — see [[Early Christianity in the Middle East]]

The Temple's destruction by Rome in **70 CE**, following the failed First Jewish-Roman War, ended Temple-centered sacrificial worship permanently and removed the institutional basis of Sadducean authority; the failed **Bar Kokhba revolt** (132–135 CE) against Rome further reshaped Jewish political and religious life in the land of Israel.

### Post-Temple / Rabbinic Judaism
After the Temple's destruction, rabbinic interpretation becomes central — a decisive transformation, not a minor adjustment, in which sacrifice is replaced by prayer, study, and legal observance as the primary structures of religious life.

The traditional narrative credits **Rabbi Yohanan ben Zakkai** with securing Roman permission to establish an academy at **Yavneh** shortly after 70 CE, from which rabbinic authority is said to have been reorganized and consolidated. Modern historical scholarship treats this "Yavneh" story with considerably more caution than older accounts did: Jacob Neusner's work reframed rabbinic literature as reflecting one Judaism among several competing "Judaisms" rather than a single continuous institutional voice, and Shaye J. D. Cohen's influential analysis reinterprets Yavneh's significance as marking a shift from sectarian exclusion toward a more pluralistic rabbinic culture of internal debate — not, as earlier tellings had it, a single dramatic founding moment that immediately produced a unified rabbinic authority over all Jewish communities. Other scholars (working from epigraphic and archaeological evidence) have noted a lack of evidence that rabbis held broad communal authority for centuries after Yavneh; some date the emergence of rabbis as the dominant religious authority considerably later, into late antiquity.

The **Mishnah**, a foundational code organizing Jewish oral law by topic, was compiled and redacted under **Judah ha-Nasi** ("Judah the Prince," c. 135–217 CE) around 200 CE. Subsequent generations of rabbinic discussion and debate on the Mishnah produced two distinct bodies of commentary (*Gemara*), combined with the Mishnah into two separate Talmuds: the **Jerusalem Talmud** (*Talmud Yerushalmi*), compiled primarily in the academies of Roman Palestine (Tiberias, Caesarea, Sepphoris — not actually Jerusalem) and generally dated to around the early-to-mid 5th century CE, and the **Babylonian Talmud** (*Talmud Bavli*), compiled in the Sasanian Persian academies and generally dated to around 500 CE or somewhat later. The Babylonian Talmud became — and remains — the more authoritative and widely studied of the two within later rabbinic Judaism.

### Diaspora and continuity
Jewish life develops across many lands while retaining powerful ties to scripture, ritual calendar, memory, law, and communal belonging. Major historical diaspora communities developed distinct liturgical, legal, and cultural traditions while sharing core textual authority: **Ashkenazi** Jewry (northern/central/eastern Europe), **Sephardi** Jewry (the Iberian Peninsula before the 1492 expulsion, and its subsequent Mediterranean and Ottoman diaspora), and **Mizrahi** Jewry (Middle Eastern and North African communities with continuous roots predating Islam) are the broadest such groupings, each with further internal regional variation.

### A challenge from within: Karaite Judaism
Not every Jewish community accepted rabbinic authority. **Karaite Judaism**, associated with the 8th-century Babylonian figure **Anan ben David** (traditionally credited as founder, though the movement's early history and Anan's precise role are debated), rejects the authority of the Talmud and rabbinic oral law, holding the written Hebrew Bible alone as authoritative — a "Scripture-only" position that produced its own independent legal and liturgical tradition, distinct communities, and centuries of polemical exchange with Rabbanite (Talmud-accepting) Judaism. Karaite communities, though today a small minority within world Jewry, still exist. Their persistence is a useful corrective to any account that treats rabbinic Judaism as the sole legitimate continuation of Second Temple Judaism rather than one successful current among competing ones.

### Medieval philosophy and mysticism
Two further major currents shape medieval and early modern Judaism. Jewish philosophical rationalism reached its most influential expression in **Moses Maimonides** (Rambam, 1138–1204), whose *Guide for the Perplexed* attempted a systematic synthesis of Jewish revelation with Aristotelian philosophy, and whose legal code (the *Mishneh Torah*) remains a major reference work. Alongside and partly in tension with this rationalist current, Jewish mysticism developed its own sophisticated theosophical tradition, **Kabbalah** — see [[../Europe/Jewish Kabbalah in Depth|Jewish Kabbalah in Depth]] for the full historical treatment, including the *Zohar*'s composition (traditionally attributed to the 2nd-century sage Shimon bar Yochai, but identified by modern philological scholarship, following Gershom Scholem, as substantially the work of the 13th-century Castilian kabbalist Moses de León and his circle).

From this mystical current, **Hasidism** emerged in 18th-century Eastern Europe around **Israel ben Eliezer**, known as the **Baal Shem Tov** ("Master of the Good Name," c. 1700–1760), emphasizing joyful, accessible devotional experience, charismatic leadership (the *tzaddik* or *rebbe*), and the possibility of encountering the divine in ordinary life, in some tension with the more text-and-study-centered rabbinic mainstream it emerged alongside. Hasidic communities remain a significant and visible part of contemporary Orthodox Judaism.

### Modern movements
The **Haskalah** ("Jewish Enlightenment," late 18th–19th century) promoted secular education, integration into surrounding European society, and critical reappraisal of tradition, generating the social and intellectual conditions from which the major modern Jewish denominational movements emerged: **Reform Judaism** (originating in early-19th-century Germany, emphasizing ethical content over ritual law and adapting practice to modern life), **Orthodox Judaism** (a self-conscious reassertion and further systematization of traditional rabbinic law and practice partly formed in reaction against Reform), and **Conservative Judaism** (a later, primarily American movement seeking a middle position — maintaining halakhic framework while allowing for historical-critical scholarship and gradual change). **Reconstructionist Judaism**, developed by Mordecai Kaplan in 20th-century America, represents a further, more explicitly naturalist reworking of Jewish theology and practice. These denominational lines are most fully institutionalized in American and, to varying degrees, other diaspora Jewish life; they map only loosely onto religious life in Israel, where the state-recognized Orthodox rabbinate and secular/traditional identity operate on different institutional terms.

## Core elements
### Torah
Torah is not only a book category.
It is law, instruction, covenantal orientation, and a central organizing principle of Jewish life. Rabbinic tradition distinguishes the **Written Torah** (the Five Books of Moses, and by extension the wider Tanakh) from the **Oral Torah** — the interpretive tradition rabbinic Judaism holds was transmitted alongside the written text and eventually recorded in the Mishnah and Talmud. This written/oral distinction is itself a rabbinic theological claim about the origin and authority of interpretation, not a neutral description all Jewish communities have accepted equally — Karaite Judaism's rejection of it, discussed above, is the clearest historical counter-example.

### Covenant
The relation between God and Israel is foundational.
This is a communal, historical, and ethical relationship, not merely an abstract belief formula.

### Law and interpretation
Judaism is one of the strongest examples in world religion of a tradition in which law and interpretation become central modes of religious life.
That means commentary, argument, and transmission matter enormously. **Halakha** (Jewish law, from a root meaning "to walk" or "the path") is the cumulative legal tradition derived from Torah, rabbinic interpretation, and subsequent codification (including Maimonides's *Mishneh Torah* and the later *Shulchan Aruch* of Joseph Karo, 16th century); **aggadah** refers to the non-legal narrative, ethical, and theological material woven throughout rabbinic literature alongside it. See [[../Cross-Regional/Sacred Law and Legalism|Sacred Law and Legalism]] for how this compares with other traditions where law occupies a similarly central religious role, including Islamic *sharia* (see [[Islam Overview]]).

### Peoplehood and memory
Judaism cannot be reduced to belief alone.
Peoplehood, ritual time, memory, sacred history, and communal continuity are all central.

## Ritual and communal life
Jewish life is shaped by:
- Sabbath (*Shabbat*) observance, weekly rather than annual, and structurally central to Jewish ritual time in a way comparable to (and worth reading alongside) [[Islam Ritual Calendar]]'s treatment of Friday prayer as a recurring rather than annual structuring rite
- festivals and sacred time — see [[../Cross-Regional/Festivals and Sacred Time|Festivals and Sacred Time]] for the wider comparative pattern; the annual cycle includes Passover, Shavuot, and Sukkot (the three pilgrimage festivals rooted in Second Temple practice), the Days of Awe (Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur), and later-instituted observances such as Hanukkah and Purim
- dietary practices (*kashrut*, "kosher" observance) and purity-related practices in some contexts
- prayer, study, and communal gathering, typically centered on the synagogue as the institution that developed to replace Temple-centered worship
- life-cycle ritual (circumcision/*brit milah*, bar/bat mitzvah, marriage, mourning practices)
- law as daily structure rather than only doctrinal statement

## Diversity within Judaism
Judaism includes multiple internal currents and historical forms.
A serious overview should leave room for:
- temple and priestly pasts
- Second Temple sectarian diversity (Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes) and its Karaite echo
- rabbinic Judaism, itself the product of a gradual consolidation rather than a single founding moment
- mystical currents (Kabbalah, Hasidism)
- legal and philosophical traditions (Maimonidean rationalism and its critics)
- modern denominational variation (Orthodox, Conservative, Reform, Reconstructionist) alongside secular and cultural Jewish identity
- regional and historical variation (Ashkenazi, Sephardi, Mizrahi, and smaller communities such as Ethiopian Beta Israel and Indian Jewish communities)

## Why it matters comparatively
Judaism is one of the most important traditions for studying:
- scripture and canon, and the written/oral distinction as a specific theological claim about interpretive authority
- revelation and law, and how a tradition can center law as intensely as belief
- memory and identity under diaspora
- commentary traditions — the Talmud's own layered, dialogical form (Mishnah plus centuries of recorded argument) is itself a distinctive model of how a tradition can treat disagreement as preserved and generative rather than something to be resolved and discarded
- ritual time and sacred community
- how a modern nation-state's religious establishment (Israel's Orthodox rabbinate) and diaspora denominational pluralism can diverge sharply within what is nonetheless one tradition

## Good working summary
Judaism combines text, law, memory, peoplehood, ritual, and interpretation in ways that cannot be reduced to belief alone. It developed through identifiable, contested historical stages — polytheistic and henotheistic roots, Second Temple sectarian plurality, a gradual rather than instantaneous rabbinic consolidation, medieval philosophical and mystical elaboration, and modern denominational diversification — rather than as one continuous, internally uniform tradition from a single founding moment.
