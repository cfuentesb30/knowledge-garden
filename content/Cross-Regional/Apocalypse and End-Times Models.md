# Apocalypse and End-Times Models

## Scope
This note compares how religious traditions imagine cosmic endings, final judgment, cyclical destruction, world renewal, hidden last battles, and the unveiling of ultimate order — and traces where these models can and can't be dated, and where scholars disagree about transmission and influence between traditions.

## Why it matters
End-times thinking is not just about panic or prediction. It reveals how a tradition understands justice, history, corruption, redemption, and the final meaning of time itself. It also has a specific technical meaning scholars distinguish from "eschatology" more broadly: apocalyptic literature, strictly defined, is a literary genre involving a revelatory disclosure (usually through a heavenly mediator, often to a named visionary) of hidden cosmic or historical truths, frequently including the imminent end of the current age — the word "apocalypse" comes from the Greek apokalypsis, "unveiling," and the genre is defined by that unveiling structure as much as by its content about the future.

---

## Core patterns

### Final judgment models
History moves toward a decisive reckoning in which the righteous and the wicked are separated, judged, vindicated, or condemned — the dominant pattern in Jewish, Christian, and Islamic eschatology, though (as with afterlife models generally) the details of how, when, and by what criteria judgment happens vary considerably even within each of those traditions.

### Cyclical destruction and renewal
Some traditions imagine repeated ages, dissolutions, or cosmic cycles rather than one unrepeatable linear ending — Hindu cosmological time and Norse mythological time are the clearest examples below, and represent a structurally different relationship to "ending" than the linear, one-time models above.

### Hidden conflict unveiling
Apocalyptic traditions often reveal that current history is the surface of a deeper struggle among divine, demonic, cosmic, or moral forces — this is central to Jewish and Christian apocalyptic literature specifically (Daniel, Revelation) and to Zoroastrian cosmic dualism.

### Catastrophe as purification
Destruction may be framed not only as punishment but as necessary clearing for renewal — a pattern that appears across linear and cyclical models alike.

## Comparative case studies

### Jewish apocalyptic literature: Daniel and beyond
The Book of Daniel, particularly its visionary second half (chapters 7–12), is widely dated by critical biblical scholarship to around 165 BCE, composed during the Maccabean revolt against the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes — a dating based on the text's detailed and accurate "predictions" of events up to that point in history followed by a shift to vaguer, less accurate predictions afterward, a pattern scholars read as evidence of the actual date of composition (the author's real historical present) versus the narrative's claimed setting centuries earlier during the Babylonian exile. This does not settle, and scholars do not claim it settles, the text's religious significance for later Jewish and Christian readers, who received it as genuinely predictive prophecy — this is a clear case where the etic (historical-critical, dating-based) and emic (traditional, revelatory) accounts diverge and both need to be stated rather than one silently overriding the other. Daniel became foundational for later Second Temple apocalyptic literature (much of it outside the eventual biblical canon, including large portions of 1 Enoch and the Dead Sea Scrolls material from Qumran), which developed increasingly elaborate accounts of angelic mediation, cosmic history, and final judgment.

### Christianity: the Book of Revelation and its contested dating
The New Testament's Book of Revelation (Apocalypse of John) is the paradigmatic apocalyptic text for later Western culture. Its dating is genuinely contested among scholars, not merely among popular interpreters: the majority scholarly position, following an early attestation from Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180 CE), dates the text to around 95–96 CE, during the reign of the emperor Domitian, reading its concerns about persecution and economic exclusion as consistent with Domitian-era pressure on Christians to participate in imperial cult; a significant minority position argues for an earlier date, in the 60s CE under Nero, based on internal textual evidence some scholars read as pointing to the Jerusalem Temple still standing at the time of writing (the Temple was destroyed in 70 CE) and other historical-allusion arguments.

The identity of "John" the author is separately contested and has been since antiquity — the 3rd-century bishop Dionysius of Alexandria already argued on stylistic grounds that the author was unlikely to be John the Apostle, a question modern scholarship continues to debate (proposals include John the Apostle, an otherwise unknown "John the Elder," and other candidates) without full consensus. See [[../Europe/Christianity in Europe Overview|Christianity in Europe Overview]].

### Zoroastrian eschatology and the question of influence on Judaism
Zoroastrian cosmology describes history as a cosmic struggle between the forces of the good creation (associated with Ahura Mazda) and the destructive spirit (Angra Mainyu/Ahriman), culminating in Frashokereti — a final restoration and "rehabilitation" of creation, associated with the arrival of a coming savior figure, the Saoshyant, and including a final judgment and resurrection of the dead. Because Zoroastrianism was the dominant religion of the Persian Empire during the period of Jewish exile and subsequent Persian imperial rule over Judea (roughly 6th–4th centuries BCE), a significant and long-running scholarly question is whether Zoroastrian eschatological ideas influenced the development of Jewish (and through it, Christian and Islamic) apocalyptic and resurrection belief.

This is genuinely contested, not settled: Mary Boyce, a leading modern scholar of Zoroastrianism, argued the parallels (final judgment, bodily resurrection, cosmic dualism, a savior figure) are close enough in structure and historically well-timed enough to indicate real influence, and went so far as to argue Zoroastrian doctrines have had more influence on humanity, directly and indirectly, than any other single system of belief. Other scholars, including Lester Grabbe, are considerably more cautious, arguing that the case for direct influence is often overstated, that many proposed parallels can be explained by independent development or by both traditions drawing on a shared, older Near Eastern/Indo-Iranian conceptual pool, and that the very late final compilation of Zoroastrian scripture (the Avesta was not written down in its current form until the Sasanian period, many centuries after the Persian period in question, raising source-critical questions about how confidently the developed Frashokereti doctrine can be projected backward to the exact centuries when contact with Judaism occurred) complicates any confident influence claim. This remains an open question in the field and should not be presented as resolved in either direction. See [[../Middle East/Zoroastrianism|Zoroastrianism]].

### Islam: signs of the Hour, the Dajjal, and the Mahdi
Islamic eschatology (al-ākhira, the afterlife/end times more broadly) includes Qur'anically attested final judgment and resurrection, elaborated in hadith literature with a detailed sequence of "signs of the Hour" preceding the end.

Two figures central to popular and hadith-based Islamic eschatology — al-Masih ad-Dajjal (a deceiving false-messiah figure) and the Mahdi (a divinely guided restorer of justice) — are treated differently across the Sunni/Shi'a division: both branches affirm belief in the Dajjal, with substantial detail in canonical Sunni hadith collections (notably Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim); the Mahdi figure is present in both traditions but functions very differently — in mainstream Sunni eschatology the Mahdi is a future, not-yet-born or not-yet-identified figure of comparatively secondary importance relative to the expected return of Jesus (ʿIsa), while in Twelver Shi'a Islam the Mahdi is identified specifically with the Twelfth Imam, understood to already have been born in the 9th century CE and to have entered a state of divinely ordained "occultation" (ghayba) from which he will one day return — a doctrine with major structural consequences for Twelver Shi'a religious authority in the interim (see [[../Middle East/Shia Islam|Shia Islam]]). See also [[../Middle East/Islam Overview|Islam Overview]].

### Hindu traditions: cyclical time and the Kali Yuga
Classical Hindu cosmology, developed across the Puranic literature, describes cosmic time as cyclical rather than linear: a great cycle (mahayuga) divides into four descending ages (Satya/Krita, Treta, Dvapara, and Kali Yuga), each shorter than the last and each marked by declining dharma (righteousness/cosmic order). Hindu tradition generally holds that the present age is Kali Yuga, traditionally reckoned by many calendrical systems as beginning around 3102 BCE and lasting many thousands of years beyond the present. At the close of Kali Yuga, tradition holds that Vishnu will incarnate as Kalki, a final avatar depicted riding a white horse and wielding a sword, to destroy corruption and inaugurate the return of Satya Yuga, restarting the cycle. This is a structurally different kind of "end" than the Abrahamic models above: not a single unrepeatable termination of history but one turn in an endlessly repeating cosmic wheel — a genuine and important point of comparative contrast, not a minor variation on the same theme. See [[../Asia/Hindu Traditions Overview|Hindu Traditions Overview]].

### Buddhism: the decline of the dharma and the future Buddha Maitreya
Buddhist traditions across multiple schools developed doctrines of a gradual decline in the efficacy and understanding of the Buddha's teaching (dharma) after his death, typically schematized into stages of decreasing duration and increasing corruption — in the East Asian elaboration most influential in Japan, three ages: the age of the "true dharma" (shōbō), the age of the "semblance dharma" (zōbō), and the final degenerate age, mappō. Buddhist studies scholar Jan Nattier's influential study *Once Upon a Future Time* (1991) demonstrated that this specific three-stage decline schema, and especially the mappō/"latter age" concept so central to later Japanese Buddhism (particularly the Pure Land and Nichiren schools), was substantially the product of Chinese Buddhist commentators rather than a direct inheritance from Indian Buddhist scripture — an important etic finding about the historical development of a doctrine that functions, within the traditions that hold it, as inherited teaching rather than later innovation. The doctrine of decline is paired with the expectation of a future Buddha, Maitreya, who will appear at a future time (variously calculated, including estimates in the billions of years) to renew the teaching — functioning within Buddhist eschatology as a restoration figure loosely comparable in narrative role, though not doctrinal content, to messianic and Mahdi-type figures in other traditions. See [[../Asia/Buddhism Overview|Buddhism Overview]].

### Norse mythology: Ragnarök
Norse cosmology, as preserved primarily in the Eddic poem *Völuspá* (a central source within the *Poetic Edda*, surviving in the 13th-century Codex Regius manuscript though the poem itself is generally judged older) and in Snorri Sturluson's 13th-century *Prose Edda*, describes Ragnarök ("fate/doom of the gods") — a final battle in which most of the major gods (Odin, Thor, Freyr, and others) die fighting monstrous forces (the wolf Fenrir, the serpent Jörmungandr, and others), the world is destroyed by fire and flood, and — in at least some versions of the text — a renewed, green earth subsequently rises from the sea, repopulated by a small number of surviving gods and two human survivors.

Scholars debate how to read this: H. R. Ellis Davidson read Ragnarök as a "dramatic expression of the precariousness of the cosmos" (order versus chaos), while other scholars, including John Lindow, treat it as an "eschatological matrix" through which Viking-Age society processed cyclical destruction generally. A further genuine complication: because *Völuspá* survives only in Christian-era Icelandic manuscripts, and because Iceland's official conversion to Christianity is traditionally dated to 999/1000 CE, scholars increasingly debate how much of the poem's specific apocalyptic and renewal imagery reflects pre-Christian Norse belief largely intact versus how much reflects conscious or unconscious Christian influence (a Judgment Day-shaped narrative arc) on a Christian-era poet working with older mythological material — recent scholarship, per current research, increasingly reads the poem as itself a product of the Christian Middle Ages in Iceland rather than a straightforward transcript of pre-Christian pagan belief. See [[../Europe/Norse Mythology Overview|Norse Mythology Overview]] and [[../Europe/Eddas and Norse Primary Sources|Eddas and Norse Primary Sources]].

## Why apocalypse matters politically
Apocalyptic thought can comfort communities experiencing persecution or oppression (a major driver of both Jewish apocalyptic literature under Seleucid and Roman rule and early Christian apocalyptic literature under Roman persecution); intensify radical or millenarian movements convinced the end is imminent and current authority illegitimate; delegitimize existing rulers by recasting them as beast/empire figures in a cosmic drama already heading toward their defeat; and turn history into moral theater with clear, cosmically guaranteed final stakes. This is why apocalyptic and millenarian movements recur so persistently in crisis periods across very different traditions and eras — from Second Temple Jewish apocalypticism under foreign occupation, to medieval and early modern Christian millenarian movements, to 19th-century movements like the Millerites (whose specific 1844 predicted date for Christ's return, the "Great Disappointment," became a formative and cautionary case study in the sociology of failed prophecy), to contemporary apocalyptic currents within multiple traditions.

## Important caution
Apocalypse, strictly and etymologically, is not only or even primarily prediction — it is unveiling. The term points to revealed hidden truth about the structure and destiny of reality, a genre concerned as much with disclosing the true (hidden) nature of the present moment as with forecasting the future. Popular usage has narrowed "apocalypse" to mean "catastrophic ending," which captures only part of what the genre and its comparative religious function actually involve.

## Comparative questions
- Is the ending linear and final, or cyclical and repeating — and what does that difference imply about how the tradition understands history's purpose?
- Who receives the apocalyptic disclosure, and through what mediating figure (angel, prophet, vision)?
- Is judgment individual, collective, cosmic, or some combination?
- What historical and political pressures (persecution, foreign rule, social crisis) can be identified around a given apocalyptic text's composition, and how does that context relate to — without simply reducing — its religious content?
- Where a possible cross-tradition influence is proposed (e.g. Zoroastrian influence on Jewish apocalypticism), what is the actual evidentiary basis, and is it treated by the field as established or as contested?

## Good working summary
End-times models are religious maps of ultimate justice and ultimate time. Whether linear or cyclical, they reveal what a tradition thinks history is for, how evil is judged, and how the world is finally made meaningful — and in nearly every case examined here, the doctrine as popularly known today has a real, datable, and sometimes contested developmental history rather than being a fixed, always-already-complete revelation.
