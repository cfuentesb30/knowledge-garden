# Comparative Religion Methods

## What it is
This note explains how to compare religions without flattening them into clichés or forcing false equivalence — and surveys how the discipline of comparative religion itself has changed, since its own history contains most of the mistakes it now warns against. Comparative religion is not a neutral toolkit applied from nowhere; it has a specific, traceable intellectual history, much of it shaped by 19th-century European assumptions this project tries not to reproduce.

## A short history of the discipline
**Friedrich Max Müller**, a German-born philologist working at Oxford, is often credited as the founder of the academic "science of religion" (*Religionswissenschaft*), a term he coined in his 1873 *Introduction to the Science of Religion*. His famous line — "he who knows one [religion] knows none," borrowed in substance from Goethe — states the discipline's founding premise: a single tradition studied in isolation cannot be understood as a religion at all, only comparison reveals what is general and what is specific to it. Müller's own comparative method was built on comparative philology (his own specialty was Sanskrit) and on a now-dated evolutionary framework that ranked religions by a presumed developmental sequence, with Victorian Protestant Christianity typically placed at or near the top — an example, still worth naming plainly, of comparison serving as a vehicle for hierarchy rather than genuinely neutral analysis.

The early-to-mid 20th century "phenomenology of religion" — **Rudolf Otto** (*The Idea of the Holy*, 1917, with its account of the numinous as *mysterium tremendum et fascinans*), **Gerardus van der Leeuw**, and later **Mircea Eliade** at the University of Chicago — tried to identify cross-culturally recurring structures of religious experience (the sacred, hierophany, myth of the eternal return) largely by setting aside historical and social context in favor of typological patterns. This approach produced influential, still widely read scholarship, but by the late 20th century it drew sustained methodological criticism for treating "the sacred" as a stable, universal category that could be studied ahistorically — in effect assuming the very thing a rigorous comparison should be testing.

## The methodological critique (why this note exists)

### Religion as a scholar's category, not a native one
**Jonathan Z. Smith**, the most influential critic of mid-century comparative method, argued in *Imagining Religion: From Babylon to Jonestown* (1982) that "religion" is not a category that exists "out there" waiting to be described — it is a second-order, scholarly construct, invented for the intellectual purposes of study and comparison, and imposed on data that often did not organize itself that way for the people who produced it. In *Drudgery Divine* (1990), Smith showed concretely how this plays out: the long scholarly tradition of comparing early Christianity to the pagan "mystery religions" of late antiquity was itself shaped by a Protestant-Catholic polemic, in which Protestant scholars sought to explain Catholic ritual, sacrament, and hierarchy as later "pagan" corruption of an originally simple, non-ritualist early Christianity. The comparison was not neutral data-gathering; it was doing theological work for one side of a Reformation-era argument, centuries after the fact and largely without saying so.

### Religion as a Western, post-Enlightenment category entangled with power
**Talal Asad**, in *Genealogies of Religion* (1993), pushes further: he argues that "religion" as a supposedly universal, cross-culturally applicable category (belief-centered, privately held, separable from politics and law) is itself a specifically modern, largely Protestant Christian construction projected outward onto other traditions that may organize practice, law, and authority quite differently. Asad's genealogical method (drawn from Nietzsche and Foucault) treats the very act of defining "religion" as inseparable from the history of power — colonial administration, missionary translation, and academic classification — that produced the definition in the first place.

### The "world religions" canon itself is a 19th–20th century construction
**Tomoko Masuzawa**, in *The Invention of World Religions* (2005), traces how the older Western fourfold scheme (Christian / Jewish / Muslim / "pagan," i.e., everything else) was replaced over the 19th and early 20th centuries by the now-familiar list of discrete "world religions" (Christianity, Islam, Judaism, Hinduism, Buddhism, and a shifting supporting cast), crystallizing into something like its current textbook form by the 1920s–30s. Her argument is not that this newer scheme is simply more accurate than the old one — it is that the "world religions" framework, despite its more pluralist and inclusive appearance, still quietly preserves European (and specifically Christian) universalism as the implicit measuring stick, and that new racial and linguistic classifications (Aryan/Semitic) shaped which traditions got elevated to "world religion" status and which were left as ethnographic curiosities. This project's own [[Overview|Cross-Regional Overview]] and regional structure exist partly to work against the version of this bias that treats Africa, the Americas, and new religious movements as secondary to a default "world religions" canon — see also the bias checks in this project's research method.

### The colonial construction of specific traditions
This is not only a claim about the category "religion" in the abstract — it applies to specific traditions treated in this garden. **Richard King**, in *Orientalism and Religion: Postcolonial Theory, India and "the Mystic East"* (1999), extends Edward Said's critique of Orientalism specifically into religious studies, arguing that "Hinduism" as a single, unified world religion (as opposed to a highly diverse family of regional traditions, deity cults, and philosophical schools) was substantially consolidated during the colonial period — not purely imposed by outsiders, King is careful to note, but co-produced by British administrators and scholars *and* by elite, often Brahmin, Indian reformers who had their own reasons to present a unified "Hinduism" to a colonial and global audience. A parallel scholarly conversation exists around the modern construction of "Buddhism" as a text-centered, philosophically rationalist religion largely by 19th-century European Buddhologists working from translated texts rather than from observing living Buddhist practice, which produced a version of "Buddhism" that many actual Buddhist practitioners across Asia would not fully recognize as a description of their own religious life. None of this means "Hinduism" or "Buddhism" are not real, lived, meaningful traditions — they obviously are, for hundreds of millions of people — it means the *specific way* Western scholarship categorized and bounded them is itself a historical event worth knowing about, not a simple discovery of a pre-existing fact.

### A working manifesto for critical distance
**Bruce Lincoln**'s "Theses on Method" (1996), thirteen short methodological propositions written for the American Academy of Religion, are among the most cited statements of the etic, historical-critical stance this project tries to practice. Lincoln's central claim is that "History" and "Religion" pull in different directions as objects of study: religious discourse characteristically claims transcendent, eternal authority for itself, while historical-critical method claims only the more modest, revisable authority of rigorous evidence and argument — and a scholar who simply reproduces a tradition's own transcendent truth-claims in the scholar's own voice, rather than analyzing why and how those claims are made, has stopped doing history and started doing theology. Lincoln is explicit that this critical distance is often experienced by believers, and sometimes by other scholars, as disrespectful "reductionism" — a tension worth naming rather than pretending away, since it is precisely the tension this project's stated *epoché* (bracketing metaphysical truth claims) is designed to hold.

## Core method
A good comparison asks:
- What specific problem or process is actually being compared — cosmology, ritual, institutional structure, law, ethics, textual authority, political theology — rather than "religion in general"?
- Are the categories genuinely equivalent, or is one side of the comparison being measured against the other's own home terms? (Comparing Christian "faith" to another tradition's ritual orthopraxy, for instance, silently imports a belief-centered definition of religion that may not fit the second tradition at all.)
- Is the comparison historical, theological, ritual, political, social, or symbolic — each of these produces a different, non-interchangeable kind of claim?
- Are we comparing elite doctrine, everyday lived practice, or institutional form — since these often diverge sharply within a single tradition, let alone across two?

## Common mistakes
- Treating all religions as ultimately saying the same underlying thing (a residue of the 19th–20th century "perennialist" assumption that all religions point to one common mystical core — itself a specific, contestable theological position, not a neutral finding)
- Comparing surface similarities (a flood myth, a dying-and-rising figure, a virgin birth motif) while ignoring the different structural role each plays within its own tradition
- Projecting modern categories backward — applying concepts like "religion," "belief," "orthodoxy," or "the individual" to societies that did not organize experience along those lines
- Assuming one tradition (usually the researcher's own, or whichever tradition dominates the conventional "world religions" canon) is the unmarked default against which everything else is described as a variant, deviation, or "syncretic" mixture — see [[Syncretism Overview]] for how this specific bias operates around that term
- Applying more scrutiny (demanding harder evidence, using more skeptical language) to indigenous, oral, diasporic, or new religious movements than to text-heavy traditions with long institutional pedigrees, or the reverse — treating a "world religion" as self-evidently rational and a smaller or newer tradition as self-evidently exotic

## Better comparison lenses
Ninian Smart's widely used "dimensions of religion" model (developed across several works from the 1960s–1990s) proposes comparing traditions along a consistent set of dimensions rather than by vague overall impression — commonly listed as: **ritual/practical**, **experiential/emotional**, **narrative/mythic**, **doctrinal/philosophical**, **ethical/legal**, **social/institutional**, and **material** (art, architecture, sacred objects). This is a genuinely useful heuristic checklist, and this project uses a version of it implicitly across its own note structure (cosmology, ritual, institution/authority, text/transmission, law, afterlife, and so on). It is worth using with one meta-level caution in mind: the framework itself still presupposes that "religion" is a coherent enough cross-cultural category to have dimensions at all — which is precisely the assumption Smith, Asad, and Masuzawa ask scholars to keep examining rather than take for granted.

Useful lenses drawn from that model and this project's own notes:
- cosmology and mythic structure
- ritual practice and its social function
- institution and authority (see [[Scripture Canon and Authority]] and [[Priests, Monks, Shamans, and Ritual Specialists]])
- sacred text and its mode of transmission (see [[Scripture vs Oral Tradition]])
- models of salvation, liberation, or right relationship with the sacred
- relation to state, law, and political legitimacy (see [[Sacred Law and Legalism]] and [[Kingship and Divine Legitimacy]])
- views of the person, soul, and afterlife (see [[Afterlife Models Across Traditions]])
- material and geographic expression (see [[Sacred Geography and Pilgrimage]])

## Good working summary
Comparative religion is useful when it sharpens distinctions as much as similarities — and it is at its most dangerous exactly when it feels most neutral, since the discipline's own history shows how easily "objective" comparison can quietly reproduce a hierarchy (evolutionary, theological, colonial) that was never actually neutral to begin with. Naming that history is part of doing the method honestly, not a distraction from it.
