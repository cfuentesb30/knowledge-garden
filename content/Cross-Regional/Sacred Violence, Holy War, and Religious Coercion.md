# Sacred Violence, Holy War, and Religious Coercion

## Scope
This note compares how religions justify, limit, ritualize, condemn, or sacralize violence, coercion, war, punishment, and forced conformity. It covers ancient and modern cases, across the same range of traditions this project treats elsewhere — with the same evidentiary standard applied whether the tradition in question is one of the conventional "world religions" or not.

## Why it matters
Any serious religion library that discusses transcendence but not coercion is dishonest. Religions can heal, restrain, and humanize. They can also authorize conquest, punishment, exclusion, and sanctified violence — sometimes the same tradition doing both at different moments, or even at the same moment for different actors within it. Treating this as a marginal footnote to "real" religious history, rather than a recurring structural feature of it, is itself a bias worth naming and avoiding.

---

## Core distinctions

### Sacred violence
Violence framed as serving divine order, cosmic battle, sacred purity, covenant, or ultimate justice — violence that draws its legitimacy from religious meaning rather than (or in addition to) ordinary political or military justification.

### Holy war
Organized conflict legitimated through religious language, symbols, law, duty, prophecy, or eschatology — a more specific and institutionally organized category than sacred violence in general, usually involving explicit religious authorization (a cleric's blessing, a legal ruling, a prophetic mandate) for a defined campaign.

### Religious coercion
Pressure, force, punishment, social exclusion, or law used to produce conformity in belief, ritual, or communal belonging — this covers a much broader range than battlefield violence, including inquisition, forced conversion, blasphemy and apostasy law, and social/economic pressure short of physical force.

## Theoretical frameworks

### Girard: the scapegoat mechanism
Anthropologist and literary theorist **René Girard**, most fully in *Violence and the Sacred* (1972), argued that archaic religion's sacrificial systems function to contain a specific social danger: unchecked "mimetic" rivalry (people wanting what others want, in escalating imitation) that threatens to spiral into generalized violence within a community. Ritual sacrifice, on this account, channels that accumulated tension onto a single substitute victim — a scapegoat — whose death (real or ritually enacted) discharges the community's violence without the group turning on itself. Girard's theory has been influential across religious studies and literary theory, but it is also a highly general, structurally totalizing claim that some anthropologists have criticized as difficult to falsify against specific ethnographic cases — worth citing as one major interpretive lens, not as an established empirical finding.

### Juergensmeyer: cosmic war
Sociologist **Mark Juergensmeyer**, in *Terror in the Mind of God* (2000, later editions), based partly on direct interviews with perpetrators and supporters of religiously framed violence across Christian, Muslim, Jewish, Sikh, and Buddhist contexts, argues that what distinguishes religiously framed violence from other political violence is the participants' sense of enacting a **cosmic war** — a transcendent, moral, ultimately meaningful battle between good and evil in which the immediate earthly conflict is a visible sign of a much larger unseen struggle. This framing, Juergensmeyer argues, is what allows acts that would otherwise be condemned even by the perpetrator's own moral code to be experienced instead as righteous, even sacred, performance.

## Doctrinal frameworks that regulate (and sometimes fail to regulate) religious violence

### Christian just war theory
**Augustine** (5th century, *City of God* and other writings) and later **Thomas Aquinas** (13th century, *Summa Theologica*) developed the framework generally known as just war theory: war may be morally permissible only under specific conditions, generally including legitimate authority (declared by a properly constituted ruler, not private individuals), just cause (typically self-defense or righting a serious wrong), and right intention (aiming at peace and justice, not conquest or hatred). This tradition has coexisted, sometimes uneasily, with periods of explicitly conquest-oriented or crusading theology that arguably exceeded its own stated limits — a tension the tradition itself has never fully resolved and continues to debate.

### Jihad: a contested and internally diverse category
Classical Islamic jurisprudence developed a substantial legal literature on *jihad* as armed struggle, including formal categories (defensive versus offensive jihad, rules of engagement, protections for non-combatants) comparable in function, though not in content, to Christian just war theory. A widely repeated popular claim — that a hadith in which Muhammad, returning from battle, calls the struggle against one's own soul the "greater jihad" and armed combat the "lesser jihad" — establishes that jihad is fundamentally an inward spiritual struggle, is itself contested within Islamic scholarship: many hadith specialists classify that specific report as weak or even fabricated in its chain of transmission, and classical legal texts overwhelmingly use "jihad" alone to mean armed struggle, reserving other vocabulary for spiritual effort. At the same time, a substantial and continuous Sufi and broader devotional tradition genuinely emphasizes internal spiritual struggle as central to religious life, with or without leaning on that specific hadith. The accurate summary is neither "jihad simply means holy war" nor "jihad simply means inner struggle" — both claims oversimplify a genuinely contested internal debate, and outside observers who confidently assert either version, in either direction, are usually reproducing a polemical rather than a scholarly position. See [[../Middle East/Islam Overview|Islam Overview]].

### Dharmic frameworks: duty, order, and righteous war
South Asian traditions developed their own vocabulary for legitimate violence, organized less around exclusivist religious truth claims than around caste duty, cosmic order, and kingship. The Bhagavad Gita's narrative frame — the warrior Arjuna's crisis of conscience on the battlefield of Kurukshetra, and Krishna's counsel that he must fight because it is his duty (dharma) as a kshatriya — is itself a foundational South Asian meditation on the ethics of righteous war (*dharmayuddha*), not a simple endorsement of violence in general. Its reception history is genuinely contested: Mohandas Gandhi read the entire text allegorically, as a portrait of the inner struggle against one's own selfish impulses rather than literal sanction for killing, a reading many traditional commentators do not share — another case, like the greater/lesser jihad debate above, where a text's violent surface narrative and its interpretive tradition pull in different directions depending on who is reading it and when.

## Comparative examples

### Ancient religion
Violence is often embedded directly in kingship, sacrifice, and conquest narrative without the later doctrinal apparatus of "holy war" as such. The biblical conquest narratives (notably in the Book of Joshua, describing the *herem* — a total destruction of enemy populations and property, dedicated to God) are a case where the etic and emic pictures diverge sharply: mainstream biblical archaeology (associated with scholars including William Dever and Israel Finkelstein, among others) has found the archaeological record largely inconsistent with a rapid military conquest of Canaan as narrated, favoring instead models of gradual settlement, internal social transformation, or a mix of processes — meaning the conquest narrative functions, for most historians, more as a later theological and national foundation story than as a straightforward campaign record, whatever normative weight it separately carries within the text's own religious tradition. See [[../Middle East/Ancient Israelite Religion|Ancient Israelite Religion]].

### Abrahamic traditions
Different periods show different combinations of restraint, just-war reasoning, conquest theology, persecution, martyrdom, and legal coercion — no single slogan captures the record for any of the three traditions.

- **The Crusades**: Pope Urban II's call at the Council of Clermont in 1095, offering participants a plenary indulgence (remission of the temporal penalty for sin) in exchange for military service to reclaim Jerusalem, launched a two-century-plus series of campaigns, not a single unified war. The Fourth Crusade (1204) notably sacked Constantinople — a Christian city — rather than reaching the Holy Land at all, a case worth naming because it shows crusading violence was not reliably contained even by its own stated religious purpose. The Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229), directed against the Cathars of southern France, and the Baltic "Northern Crusades" against still-pagan Baltic and Finnic peoples, extended crusading warfare against targets within and adjacent to Christendom rather than only against external Muslim powers. See [[../Europe/Catharism and the Albigensian Crusade|Catharism and the Albigensian Crusade]].
- **Inquisitions and religious coercion**: Medieval and early modern inquisitorial bodies (the medieval Inquisition from the 12th–13th centuries; the Spanish Inquisition, formally established 1478) prosecuted heresy, apostasy, and (in Spain) suspected insincere conversion among Jews and Muslims forced to convert. Popular accounts have historically inflated the scale of Inquisition executions dramatically; modern archival research (notably by historian Henry Kamen) has revised death-toll estimates for the Spanish Inquisition sharply downward from earlier popular claims, while still documenting real, sustained, and often brutal coercive activity across several centuries. This pattern — dramatic early or polemical death-toll claims later revised downward by archival historians, while the underlying coercive institution's reality is not in doubt — recurs across multiple traditions in this note and should be treated consistently wherever it appears, including in the Aztec case below.
- **Sikh martial tradition as a case shaped by persecution**: Guru Arjan, the fifth Sikh Guru, was executed under Mughal authority in 1606; Guru Tegh Bahadur, the ninth Guru, was executed in 1675. In 1699 Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Guru, founded the **Khalsa**, formalizing the *sant-sipahi* ("saint-soldier") ideal that combines spiritual discipline with readiness for armed self-defense. Sikh tradition's own framing of this militarization is explicitly protective and defensive — a response to persecution rather than an expansionist or missionary military program — and that self-understanding is broadly consistent with the historical circumstances of its founding, though how that ideal has been invoked in later contexts (including 20th-century Sikh separatist violence) is itself a separate and more contested later chapter. See [[../Asia/Sikhism Overview|Sikhism Overview]].

### South and East Asian settings
Violence in these settings is often framed differently from the Abrahamic pattern above — less through exclusivist truth claims about a single correct religion, and more through kingship, cosmic and social order, protection of the dharma, or state-religious synthesis. That framing difference does not mean sanctified violence is absent.
- **Ashoka** (Mauryan emperor, r. c. 268–232 BCE) is often cited as history's clearest case of a ruler's turn from conquest to religiously motivated nonviolence: his own rock and pillar edicts describe deep remorse after the brutal Kalinga war (traditionally dated to 261 BCE, with the edicts themselves citing roughly 150,000 deportations and comparable casualty figures), followed by his embrace of Buddhist-inflected *dhamma* as state policy. Historians note a genuine complication worth stating plainly: because the edicts are Ashoka's own state propaganda, not an independent record, some scholars read the Kalinga-conversion narrative more cautiously, as a partly rhetorical tool for consolidating moral authority rather than a simple, transparent autobiographical account — without denying that Ashoka's subsequent policy really did shift markedly toward non-violence and religious tolerance as state doctrine.
- **Buddhist nationalism and communal violence**: Despite Buddhism's widespread popular reputation (including in some of this project's own comparative shorthand) as an unusually nonviolent tradition, 20th- and 21st-century Buddhist-majority states have seen serious religiously inflected violence. In Sri Lanka, Sinhalese Buddhist nationalism has repeatedly fused with ethnic conflict against the Tamil minority and, more recently, organizations such as Bodu Bala Sena have targeted Muslim communities. In Myanmar, Buddhist nationalist organizations (notably the monk-led MaBaTha movement, with figures such as Ashin Wirathu prominent in anti-Muslim rhetoric) provided religious framing that scholars studying the crisis link to the environment enabling the 2016–2017 military campaign against the Rohingya Muslim minority, which the United Nations and multiple governments have characterized as ethnic cleansing or genocide. This is directly relevant to the symmetric-treatment standard this project holds itself to: a tradition's peaceful self-image or peaceful majority practice does not exempt specific historical and contemporary movements within it from the same scrutiny applied elsewhere. See [[../Asia/Buddhism Overview|Buddhism Overview]].

### Mesoamerica: a case study in contested numbers
Aztec (Mexica) state religion included human sacrifice as an integral, religiously mandated practice, understood within Mexica cosmology as necessary to sustain the sun and cosmic order. The *scale* of the practice is genuinely and significantly contested: Spanish chroniclers writing after the conquest reported enormous figures (tens of thousands in a single multi-day ceremony, by some accounts), but modern historians widely treat these numbers as likely inflated, in part because they served the conquerors' own interest in justifying the conquest and evangelization of Mexica society as a rescue from a uniquely bloodthirsty enemy. Archaeological evidence (skull racks and sacrificial remains at the Templo Mayor and elsewhere) confirms the practice was real, regular, and religiously central, while suggesting a smaller — though still substantial — scale than the most dramatic colonial-era claims. As with the Inquisition case above, the right conclusion is neither "the practice was a myth" nor "the largest reported numbers are simply accurate" — both the practice's reality and the contested, likely inflated scale of the most-cited numbers should be held together. See [[../Americas/Mesoamerican Religions Overview|Mesoamerican Religions Overview]].

### Modern movements
Apocalyptic or totalizing new religious movements can fuse a sense of unique sacred mission with coercive control or catastrophic violence — Aum Shinrikyo's 1995 sarin gas attack on the Tokyo subway, carried out by a Japanese new religious movement blending elements of Buddhist, Hindu, and Christian apocalyptic imagery around its leader's claimed enlightenment, is among the clearest and most-studied modern cases (Juergensmeyer's cosmic-war framework, above, was developed partly with cases like this in view). Christian Identity and related movements in North America, and various armed millenarian groups elsewhere, show the same pattern is not confined to any single tradition or region.

## Why comparison matters
A serious researcher should ask, in every case above:
- What exactly is being justified?
- Who has authority to declare violence or coercion legitimate, and by what process?
- What limits are claimed by the tradition's own doctrine?
- How often are those limits actually observed versus ignored in practice, and who gets to decide?
- Is the violence framed as defense, purification, punishment, liberation, or conquest — and does that framing hold up against the independent historical record, or only against the tradition's own account of itself?

## Important caution
Not all strong conviction leads to violence. Not all violence in religious settings is caused by doctrine alone — political, economic, and ethnic grievances are almost always present alongside religious framing, and disentangling "how much was really religious" from a conflict is often impossible and sometimes the wrong question. But religious language can intensify, sanctify, and protect violence from criticism in ways that purely secular political language typically cannot — by locating the conflict, as Juergensmeyer argues, within a cosmic and transcendent frame rather than an ordinary, negotiable, revisable political one. That is the key analytical point this note is built around, and it applies with the same force to every tradition surveyed above, not selectively to some and not others.

## Good working summary
Religious violence is rarely only about belief, but belief can legitimize it powerfully. The key comparative question is not which tradition is "more violent" in the abstract — a question this note deliberately does not try to answer — but how sacred authority is structured, invoked, contested, and sometimes exceeded, in specific historical cases across every tradition examined here.
