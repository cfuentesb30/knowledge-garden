# Cross-Regional Overview

## Scope
This section holds thematic notes that cut across regions and traditions. Use it for comparison, transmission, syncretism, canon formation, ritual patterns, sacred space, authority, cosmology, violence, and method. Where the regional shelves (Africa, Americas, Asia, Europe, Middle East) go deep on one tradition or cluster of traditions in its own historical context, this shelf goes wide — asking the same question (how is scripture authorized? how is time made sacred? how does syncretism happen? when does religion authorize violence?) across every region at once.

## Why cross-regional study matters
No tradition develops in isolation, and no comparative claim is trustworthy unless it is made explicitly and checked against more than one case. Two risks run in opposite directions, and this shelf exists to guard against both:
- **Treating the conventional "world religions" canon (Christianity, Islam, Judaism, Hinduism, Buddhism) as the default template**, against which African, Afro-diasporic, Indigenous American, and new religious movements get described as variations, exceptions, or "syncretic" mixtures. That canon is itself a 19th–20th century Western scholarly construction — see [[Comparative Religion Methods]].
- **Comparing traditions loosely enough that real differences disappear** — assuming every tradition has an equivalent of "faith," "scripture," "conversion," or "the individual soul" just because a familiar tradition does, when the categories themselves may not map cleanly onto how another tradition organizes practice, law, or authority.

Good cross-regional work names its comparison precisely (comparing *what*, on *what* basis) rather than gesturing at similarity in the abstract.

## Major thematic clusters
### Method and framing
How comparison itself works, and what assumptions it smuggles in when done carelessly — see [[Comparative Religion Methods]].

### Text, authority, and transmission
How communities decide what counts as scripture, who may interpret it, and whether authority runs through text, institution, oral lineage, or charisma — see [[Scripture Canon and Authority]] and [[Scripture vs Oral Tradition]].

### Time, space, and ritual pattern
How traditions structure the calendar and the landscape, and what recurring ritual forms (priesthood, sacrifice, purity, initiation) show up in comparable but non-identical shapes across otherwise unrelated traditions — see [[Festivals and Sacred Time]] and [[Sacred Geography and Pilgrimage]].

### Contact, change, and coercion
How traditions change through contact rather than staying fixed — syncretism, mission, conversion — and how religious authority can also license violence, coercion, and exclusion rather than only meaning, healing, or restraint — see [[Syncretism Overview]], [[Conversion and Mission]], and [[Sacred Violence, Holy War, and Religious Coercion]].

## Core themes worth tracking
- comparative method that sharpens distinctions instead of flattening them
- canon formation as a historical process, not a single founding event
- sacred time and sacred space as material, checkable religious phenomena, not just doctrine
- syncretism as a contested category, not a neutral description
- the recurring, uncomfortable link between sacred authority and coercive or violent power
- divine triads, kingship myths, and death/rebirth patterns as cross-traditional structures worth comparing carefully rather than assuming they mean the same thing everywhere they appear

## Research lenses that help here
- always name what specifically is being compared (cosmology, ritual, law, institution, text) rather than comparing "religions" as undifferentiated wholes
- keep emic (a tradition's own self-understanding) and etic (what historians and textual scholars can establish) explicitly distinct
- apply the same evidentiary standard and vocabulary to every tradition — a claim treated as "myth" in one tradition and "history" in a structurally similar case elsewhere is a symmetry failure, not a neutral description
- treat contested scholarship (text dating, historicity, origins, syncretism) as contested — name the positions rather than silently picking a winner

## Recommended resources
- Jonathan Z. Smith — for methodological caution about comparison itself
- Wilfred Cantwell Smith — for the relational, community-dependent nature of both "scripture" and "religion" as categories
- Tomoko Masuzawa — for the history of the "world religions" category this shelf tries not to reproduce
- Mircea Eliade — for older broad phenomenological framing, useful but to be read critically rather than as settled method
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and university comparative-religion syllabi, for tighter, more current scholarship on any single thematic note above

## Good working summary
This shelf is where the Garden's individual regional and tradition-specific notes get put in conversation with each other — carefully, on named terms, without assuming any one tradition is the template the others are measured against.
