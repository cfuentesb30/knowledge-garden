# Scripture Canon and Authority

## What it is
This note studies how religious communities decide which texts count, how those texts are interpreted, and who has authority over meaning. "Scripture" sounds like a single, stable category — a fixed set of written texts a religion treats as authoritative — but canon formation is nearly always a slow, contested, retrospectively-narrated historical process, not a single founding event, and "authority over a text" is a separate question from "possession of a text." Two traditions can hold texts that look structurally similar and still operate on completely different logics of who gets to say what those texts mean.

## Core questions
- What counts as scripture, and who decided?
- Is canon closed (a fixed, final list) or open (still capable of growing)?
- Is authority located in the text itself, in an institution that interprets it, in an unbroken chain of teachers, in inspired individuals, or in some combination?
- How do interpretation disputes — over a single word, a single ruling, a single translation choice — reshape the tradition around the text?
- What is the relationship between a text's *written* fixation and its *oral/aural* life in worship, recitation, and memorization? (See below — this is a bigger question than it first appears.)

## Why it matters
Two traditions may both have "sacred texts" and operate very differently in how authority is assigned and contested. A Quran and a New Testament are both central texts, memorized and recited both, but Islamic and Christian textual authority work through nearly opposite institutional logics — one largely without a founding ecumenical council fixing the text's boundaries, the other largely built around exactly that kind of council. Understanding canon and authority requires tracking specific historical processes, not assuming a shared template drawn from whichever tradition is most familiar to the reader.

## Canon formation: comparative cases

### Hebrew Bible / Tanakh
The Hebrew Bible's three sections (Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim) reached fixed form at different times and through different processes, not a single ratifying event. A widely repeated but now largely discredited account holds that a "Council of Jamnia" (Yavne), around 90 CE, formally closed the Hebrew canon. That story traces to 19th-century scholar Heinrich Graetz's interpretation of rabbinic sources; what those sources (chiefly the Mishnah tractate *Yadaim*) actually record is an ongoing rabbinic discussion at Yavne about the canonical status of specific contested books (notably Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes), not a single council with binding legislative authority over the whole canon. Most specialists today, including scholars who once used the "Jamnia" framework, no longer treat it as historically accurate. The Dead Sea Scrolls (mid-3rd century BCE–1st century CE) independently show that before 70 CE, Jewish communities held a more textually fluid set of scriptures than the later fixed Masoretic tradition, with some books circulating in multiple text-forms. The consonantal text was substantially stabilized earlier, but full vocalization and the authoritative Masoretic Text were the work of the Masoretes across roughly the 7th–10th centuries CE.

### Christian New Testament
The 27-book New Testament canon familiar today is the end point of roughly three centuries of contested selection, not an apostolic-era given. The mid-2nd-century teacher Marcion of Sinope proposed the earliest known *closed* Christian canon — an edited Gospel of Luke plus ten Pauline letters, explicitly excluding the entire Hebrew Bible — and was declared heretical by the wider church, partly in reaction against his canon. That controversy is one of the forces that pushed the broader church toward defining its own boundaries. The Muratorian fragment (a Latin manuscript reflecting a Greek original usually dated to the late 2nd century, c. 170–200 CE) is the earliest surviving list of New Testament books resembling something like the eventual canon, though it lists only 22 of the 27. The first surviving list matching exactly the 27 books used today appears in Athanasius of Alexandria's 39th Festal Letter (367 CE) — a bishop's pastoral letter, not a council ruling. Conciliar ratification came later still, at regional councils (Synod of Hippo, 393; Council of Carthage, 397) rather than one of the great ecumenical councils. Even after that, the boundary was not universally fixed: Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Protestant Bibles disagree to this day on the Old Testament's scope (the deuterocanonical/apocryphal books), a disagreement sharpened at the Council of Trent (1546), which formally fixed the Catholic canon partly in response to Luther's rejection of those books' full canonical status.

### Qur'an
Historical-critical and traditional Islamic accounts broadly agree on the outline: under the third caliph, Uthman ibn Affan (r. 644–656), a committee including Zayd ibn Thabit produced a standardized written text based on Hafsa bint Umar's collection, in the dialect of Quraysh, and had copies sent to major garrison cities, with variant personal codices (notably those associated with the companions Ibn Mas'ud and Ubayy ibn Ka'b, which reportedly differed in some content and arrangement) ordered destroyed to enforce uniformity. Where scholarship genuinely divides is on how much variation persisted after Uthman's standardization and how gradual the process of full stabilization was. Classical Islamic scholarship itself preserves a tradition of multiple *qira'at* (canonical reading traditions, seven or ten depending on the counting), reflecting real, sanctioned variation in vocalization and some wording within the consonantal skeleton. Some recent manuscript-based scholarship (for example, analysis of the Sana'a palimpsest, a Quranic manuscript with an erased earlier text visible beneath the standard one) has been read by some scholars as evidence of a more gradual, evolving stabilization than the traditional account suggests; other scholars read the same evidence as consistent with the traditional account once ordinary scribal variation is accounted for. This remains an active, technical, and sometimes polemicized area of manuscript scholarship rather than a settled question, and claims from confessional apologetics on either side (Muslim devotional and anti-Islamic polemical) should be treated with particular caution here.

### Buddhist canon(s)
There is no single Buddhist canon — a point worth stating plainly, since "the Buddhist scriptures" is sometimes described as if it were one thing. The Pali Canon (Tipitaka) of Theravada Buddhism was transmitted orally for roughly four centuries after the Buddha's death before being committed to writing, according to Theravada tradition around the 1st century BCE at Aluvihara in Sri Lanka, under conditions (a famine and political crisis threatening the monastic community) that motivated writing it down for preservation — an event Theravada tradition counts as the fourth Buddhist council. East Asian Buddhism transmits a much larger Chinese Buddhist Canon incorporating Mahayana sutras absent from the Pali Canon; Tibetan Buddhism has its own canon divided into the Kangyur (translated words attributed to the Buddha) and Tengyur (translated commentarial and treatise literature). These canons overlap substantially but are not identical, and the historicity of the very first council (traditionally held at Rajagriha immediately after the Buddha's death) is itself debated by historians, resting on later narrative accounts rather than contemporary documentation.

### Hindu textual authority
Hindu tradition draws a foundational internal distinction between **shruti** ("that which is heard" — the Vedas, understood in traditional terms as eternal and revealed rather than authored by any human being) and **smriti** ("that which is remembered" — the epics, Dharmashastras, and Puranas, understood as human-composed but still authoritative). There was never a single canonizing council comparable to a Christian synod or an Islamic caliphal commission; canonical authority is distributed, layered, and in important respects still open — individual sectarian traditions (Vaishnava, Shaiva, Shakta) recognize their own additional authoritative texts (Agamas, Tantras, the Bhagavad Gita's elevated status within many traditions despite technically belonging to smriti rather than shruti). What did reach an extraordinary degree of fixation is the *sound* of the Vedic text: traditional reciters developed multiple interlocking recitation methods (including *pada-patha*, reciting each word separately, and *krama-patha*, reciting words in overlapping pairs) specifically to guard against any drift in the orally transmitted text across generations — UNESCO recognized this tradition of Vedic chanting in 2003 as a Masterpiece of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The precision is a textual-fidelity achievement of a fundamentally oral kind, distinct from and arguably rivaling the fixity that other traditions pursued through writing.

### Sikh scripture as living authority
The Sikh case is unusual in making the authority question explicit rather than implicit. Guru Arjan, the fifth Guru, compiled the Adi Granth in 1604 from the hymns of his predecessors and his own compositions, installing it at the Harmandir Sahib (the Golden Temple). Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Guru, later prepared a further recension (traditionally called the Damdami Bir, c. 1706) adding the hymns of the ninth Guru, his father Guru Tegh Bahadur. In 1708, shortly before his death, Guru Gobind Singh declared the line of human Gurus ended and installed the text itself, henceforth the Guru Granth Sahib, as the eternal and living Guru of the Sikh community — a direct transfer of religious authority from a line of persons to a fixed text, treated by the tradition not merely as a book but as a living spiritual authority to be approached with the same reverence as a human Guru. See [[../Asia/Sikhism Overview|Sikhism Overview]].

### Zoroastrian Avesta
The Avesta's oldest layer (the Gathas, attributed to Zoroaster himself) and later liturgical material were transmitted orally for a very long period before being written down. Zoroastrian tradition holds that a comprehensive written compilation — organized into 21 *Nasks* — was undertaken under the Sassanian dynasty, with the process generally dated to around the 6th century CE, using a script (the Avestan alphabet) apparently devised specifically for the purpose of fixing the liturgical pronunciation accurately. Much of this compiled material was subsequently lost following the Arab conquest of Iran in the 7th century CE; only a fraction of the original 21 Nasks survives today, mostly liturgical portions still in ritual use, alongside later Pahlavi (Middle Persian) translations and commentaries that partially preserve the content of some lost sections. See [[../Middle East/Zoroastrianism|Zoroastrianism]].

## Same text, different authority structure
A genuinely useful comparative move is holding the text constant and varying the authority structure around it:
- **Sunni and Shia Islam** share the same Quranic text but diverge sharply on supplementary authoritative literature (different, though overlapping, hadith collections — the Sunni "six books" headed by *Sahih al-Bukhari* and *Sahih Muslim* versus Shia collections such as *al-Kafi*) and, more fundamentally, on where interpretive authority sits after the Prophet: a scholarly consensus-based structure in Sunni Islam versus the authority of the Imams (and, in Twelver Shia Islam, the ongoing juristic authority of the *marja'* in the absence of the Hidden Imam) in Shia Islam. See [[../Middle East/Shia Islam|Shia Islam]].
- **Rabbinic and Karaite Judaism** share the Hebrew Bible but divide over whether an Oral Torah (later codified as the Mishnah and Talmud) carries authority alongside the written text. Karaite Judaism, associated with the 8th-century figure Anan ben David, rejected rabbinic oral tradition as authoritative and insisted on scripture alone — a medieval instance of the same "text versus institutional-interpretive tradition" tension that recurs in the Protestant Reformation's *sola scriptura* centuries later.
- **Catholic and Protestant Christianity** share almost the same New Testament (differing over the Old Testament's scope, above) but divide fundamentally on whether the Bible is self-sufficiently authoritative (*sola scriptura*, the Reformation position) or requires the Church's magisterium and living tradition to authoritatively interpret it (the Catholic and Orthodox position). See [[../Europe/Protestant Reformation|Protestant Reformation]].

In each pair, the disagreement is not chiefly about which words are scripture — it is about the structure of authority that surrounds an agreed or near-agreed text. That is the analytical payoff of separating "canon" from "authority" as this note's title does.

## Oral scripture, not just written scripture
A recurring bias in how outsiders define "scripture" is to treat it as fundamentally a *written* category — a book on a shelf. Historian of religion **William A. Graham**, in *Beyond the Written Word* (1987), argues this gets the phenomenon backward for most of religious history: across traditions, a text functions as scripture primarily through its oral and aural life — recitation, chanting, memorization, liturgical performance — and this remained true for the vast majority of religious practitioners, most of whom could not read, well into the modern era. Vedic recitation, Quranic *tajwid* (the discipline of recitation), the cantillation of the Hebrew Bible, and the chanting of Buddhist sutras are not secondary applications of an essentially written text; in Graham's argument, the spoken/heard form is arguably prior, and treating "scripture" as definitionally written imports a modern, literate, print-culture assumption that distorts how most believers across history actually encountered their sacred texts. See [[Scripture vs Oral Tradition]] for the broader comparative question of oral versus written transmission.

## Study dimensions
- canon formation (open vs. closed, and how closure happened historically in each case)
- commentary traditions (Talmud, tafsir, patristic and scholastic exegesis, Vedanta commentary literature)
- translation politics (Latin Vulgate's authority in medieval Catholicism; debates over vernacular Bible translation in the Reformation; the traditional Islamic position that the Quran is fully itself only in Arabic, with translations treated as interpretation rather than the Quran proper)
- clergy/scholar authority versus lay or charismatic authority
- oral transmission versus written fixation, and what is gained or lost in each
- reform and heresy disputes as the main historical mechanism by which canon and authority questions actually get contested and resolved (or left unresolved)

## Why authority is a separate question from having a text
**Wilfred Cantwell Smith**, in *What Is Scripture? A Comparative Approach* (1993), argues that scripture is best understood as a *relational* category rather than an intrinsic property of a text: a text becomes scripture through a community's sustained treatment of it as scripture (recitation, ritual use, interpretive attention, claims of transcendent origin), not because of some feature the words themselves possess independent of that community relationship. This reframes the whole note's core questions: "what counts as scripture" is always also "who is doing the counting, and what are they doing with the text once it counts."

## Good working summary
Sacred text alone does not explain a religion. Canon formation is a historical process — contested, gradual, and often only retrospectively described as a single decisive moment — and authority is shaped by the community, its institutions, its interpreters, and the historical struggles fought around the text, not by the text's mere existence.
