# Sacred Geography and Pilgrimage

## Scope
This note compares how religions map holiness onto place: mountains, rivers, temples, shrines, tombs, cities, routes, and landscapes — and how people move through that mapped landscape as pilgrims. It also compares the scholarly theories built to explain why pilgrimage matters, since those theories disagree with each other in instructive ways.

## Why it matters
Religions do not live only in beliefs or texts. They live in geography. Sacred space tells people where divine power is concentrated, where memory is anchored, and where ritual movement matters. Because sacred geography is physical and often contested — a mountain, a city, a river shared or fought over by more than one tradition — it is also one of the places where religious claims most directly intersect with politics, sovereignty, and law, not just cosmology.

## Theoretical frameworks

### Turner: pilgrimage as rite of passage
Anthropologists **Victor** and **Edith Turner** brought Arnold van Gennep's concept of the rite of passage to pilgrimage studies, most influentially in Victor Turner's essay "The Center Out There: Pilgrims' Goal" (1973) and the later *Image and Pilgrimage in Christian Culture* (1978, with Edith Turner). Their argument: pilgrimage functions structurally like other rites of passage, moving a person through a **liminal** phase — a state of being "betwixt and between," outside ordinary social structure — that can produce **communitas**: an intense, temporary, egalitarian solidarity among pilgrims who would otherwise be separated by rank, class, or region. On this model, a pilgrimage site is "the center out there" — a place made powerful precisely by being outside the pilgrim's everyday structured social world.

### The counter-argument: pilgrimage as contested space, not unified communitas
**John Eade** and **Michael Sallnow**, in the edited volume *Contesting the Sacred: The Anthropology of Christian Pilgrimage* (1991), directly challenged the Turnerian model on ethnographic grounds: at the specific Christian pilgrimage shrines they and their contributors studied, they found competition and friction rather than the egalitarian communitas Turner's model predicted — different pilgrim groups, local clergy, tourists, and shrine authorities often pursuing incompatible meanings and interests at the same physical site simultaneously. Their alternative framing treats a pilgrimage shrine less as a generator of unity and more as an open arena capable of accommodating multiple, competing religious and social agendas at once. Both frameworks remain in active use in pilgrimage studies; which one fits better appears to depend substantially on the specific site and historical moment being studied, and scholars increasingly treat this as a real, unresolved empirical question rather than a settled theoretical dispute with one clear winner.

### Eck: sacred geography as a structured system, not a scatter of holy spots
Historian of religion **Diana Eck**, especially in *Banaras: City of Light* (1982) and *India: A Sacred Geography* (2012), developed the idea of the **tirtha** (literally a "crossing place" or ford) as a way of describing how Hindu sacred geography works: a tirtha is a specific point where movement between the human and divine worlds is understood to be unusually possible, and Hindu India is organized as a linked, replicating network of such crossing places — sacred rivers, sacred mountains, and sacred cities that echo and re-present each other (a local river may be understood as a manifestation of the Ganges; a local shrine may replicate a more distant one) rather than existing as isolated holy sites. Eck's broader argument is that this network itself constitutes a kind of unifying "idea of India" that long predates, and is not reducible to, any single political or administrative unification of the subcontinent.

## Common patterns across traditions
- temple cities and pilgrimage centers that organize an entire urban economy and civic identity around a sacred site
- mountains treated as divine dwelling places, axis points connecting earth and heaven, or liminal zones between worlds
- rivers as sources of purification, fertility, cosmic boundary, or passage between the world of the living and the dead
- tombs and relic sites, where a holy person's remains or associated objects are treated as a locus of ongoing spiritual power
- pilgrimage routes that structure long-distance travel, hospitality networks, and regional economic integration
- sacred centers used to legitimize political order — a ruler's claim to authority is frequently anchored to control of, or patronage toward, a recognized sacred site

## Comparative examples

### Mecca and the Hajj (Islam)
Even before Islam, Mecca and the Kaaba functioned as a pilgrimage destination for Arabian tribes; Islamic tradition holds that the Kaaba was built by Abraham and Ishmael and later filled with idols before Muhammad's cleansing and rededication of the site in 630 CE. Several core Hajj rituals — circumambulation of the Kaaba, running between the hills of Safa and Marwah, the symbolic stoning — have recognizable pre-Islamic antecedents that were retained and reinterpreted within an Islamic theological framework rather than invented from nothing, a pattern scholars of early Islam and of religion generally treat as normal continuity-with-reinterpretation rather than as evidence against the tradition's own account of Abrahamic origin. The Hajj, one of the Five Pillars of Islam, remains obligatory at least once in a lifetime for adult Muslims with the means to perform it, and functions today as one of the largest recurring mass gatherings of any kind in the world. See [[../Middle East/Islam Overview|Islam Overview]] and [[../Middle East/Islam Ritual Calendar|Islam Ritual Calendar]].

### Jerusalem (Judaism, Christianity, Islam)
Jerusalem is sacred, and its sacredness is claimed by more than one tradition over the same physical ground — the clearest case in this note of contested rather than merely shared sacred geography. The Temple Mount / Haram al-Sharif complex holds the Western Wall (a remnant of the Second Temple's retaining wall, Judaism's holiest accessible site) directly adjacent to al-Aqsa Mosque and the Dome of the Rock (the third-holiest site in Islam, associated with Muhammad's Night Journey), while the nearby Church of the Holy Sepulchre marks the traditional site of Jesus's crucifixion and resurrection in Christian tradition. This is not a historical curiosity — it remains one of the most consequential live instances of sacred geography generating present-day political conflict anywhere in the world. See [[../Middle East/Ancient Israelite Religion|Ancient Israelite Religion]] and [[../Middle East/Early Christianity in the Middle East|Early Christianity in the Middle East]].

### Varanasi and the Ganges (Hindu traditions)
Varanasi (Banaras/Kashi) on the Ganges is widely regarded within Hindu tradition as an unusually powerful tirtha, associated especially with Shiva; dying and being cremated there is understood by many Hindus to grant liberation (moksha) from the cycle of rebirth. Historically and today, this belief supports an entire visible urban economy of cremation ghats, pilgrimage lodging, and ritual specialists — a case where the emic claim (this specific place grants liberation) and the etic, observable fact (this specific place has functioned as a major pilgrimage and death-ritual economy for well over a thousand years) sit alongside each other without the second needing to adjudicate the first. See [[../Asia/Hindu Traditions Overview|Hindu Traditions Overview]].

### Mount Kailash (Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, and Bon traditions)
Mount Kailash in western Tibet is sacred simultaneously to four distinct traditions, each with its own account of why: Hindu tradition holds it as the abode of Shiva and Parvati; Tibetan Buddhism associates it with the tantric deity Chakrasamvara (Demchok); Jain tradition holds it as the site where the first Tirthankara, Rishabhanatha, attained liberation; and the Bon tradition (Tibet's indigenous religious system, later reshaped through contact with Buddhism) regards it as an axis mundi connecting heaven and earth. By longstanding convention among pilgrims of all four traditions — reinforced today by Chinese government restriction on the mountain — Kailash has, unlike many other high peaks, no recorded successful ascent; pilgrims circle it on foot (the Kailash kora) rather than climbing it, which is itself a notable contrast with how sacred mountains are treated in some other traditions. See [[../Asia/Tibetan Buddhism and Vajrayana|Tibetan Buddhism and Vajrayana]] and [[../Asia/Jainism|Jainism]].

### Santiago de Compostela and the Camino (Christianity)
A tomb discovered near Compostela in 813 CE was identified as that of the apostle James; the resulting shrine grew from a modest local devotion in the 9th–10th centuries into one of medieval Christendom's three great pilgrimage destinations alongside Jerusalem and Rome, particularly under the energetic promotion of Archbishop Diego Gelmírez in the early 12th century, and supported by one of the earliest surviving pilgrim guidebooks, the *Codex Calixtinus* (12th century). The Camino de Santiago has seen a substantial modern revival — as a UNESCO World Heritage route and as a destination for a mix of traditionally religious, spiritually-seeking-but-not-religious, and purely recreational walkers, a contemporary pattern worth noting on its own terms rather than treating as a simple continuation of medieval devotion.

### Lourdes (Catholic Christianity)
Between February and July 1858, a 14-year-old girl, Bernadette Soubirous, reported eighteen apparitions of a lady she later identified with the Virgin Mary at a grotto near Lourdes, France. What makes Lourdes analytically interesting beyond the apparition claim itself is the Catholic Church's own institutional epistemology for handling it: the local bishop convened a formal investigative commission within the same year, took until 1862 to declare the apparitions "worthy of belief," and in 1883 established a standing **Medical Bureau** at the shrine — staffed by physicians, including non-Catholic physicians, whose job is specifically to review claimed healings against ordinary medical explanation before the Church will consider certifying any of them as miraculous. Of several thousand healing claims reported since 1858, the Church has to date formally recognized a relatively small number (accounts vary in the low dozens to around 70 depending on how recognitions are counted) as miraculous — a deliberately conservative, evidentiary process worth noting as an emic institution that itself applies something structurally similar to a skeptical etic standard before ratifying a claim.

### Abydos and Karnak (ancient Egyptian religion)
Abydos functioned as a major pilgrimage and cenotaph site associated with the cult of Osiris, where Egyptians of means sought to be commemorated even if not actually buried there, to associate themselves with Osiris's mythic death and resurrection. The Karnak–Luxor temple complex at Thebes hosted the Opet Festival, an annual procession in which the cult image of Amun traveled between the two temple sites — an example of sacred geography activated periodically through festival movement rather than being a single fixed point. See [[../Africa/Ancient Egyptian Religion|Ancient Egyptian Religion]] and [[../Africa/Osiris, Isis, and Horus|Osiris, Isis, and Horus]].

### Andean sacred landscape
Andean religious traditions, both before and after Spanish conquest, treat specific mountains (**apu**, understood as powerful, often personified beings) and other charged locations (**huacas** — a broad category covering springs, rock outcrops, ancestral mummies, and other sites understood to hold sacred power) as active, ongoing participants in ritual and reciprocal obligation, not simply as backdrop. See [[../Americas/Andean Religions Overview|Andean Religions Overview]].

### Uluru (Anangu traditional custodianship, central Australia)
Uluru is sacred to the Anangu people as a site where ancestral beings of the Tjukurpa (creation-period narratives that also govern law and ceremony) are understood to have acted, and it contains numerous specific sacred sites — some restricted even to specific Anangu men or women under traditional law — within the larger formation. After decades of Anangu advocacy following the return of the surrounding land to Anangu ownership in 1985, a formal ban on climbing Uluru took effect in October 2019, ending a practice the traditional owners had long identified as a serious violation of the site's sanctity (compounded by erosion damage and the absence of toilet facilities along the climbing route). This is a useful comparative case precisely because it involves living custodians with legal standing successfully asserting control over how outsiders may treat their sacred site — a different power dynamic than most of the historical cases in this note, where the sacred-geography claims of colonized or displaced peoples were overridden rather than accommodated.

### The Black Hills (Lakota and other Northern Plains peoples)
The Black Hills (Lakota: *Paha Sapa*) are regarded by the Lakota, Cheyenne, and other Northern Plains peoples as sacred, central to origin narratives and ongoing ceremony. The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty recognized the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills, as reserved for the Sioux Nation; the United States nonetheless seized the Black Hills in 1877 after gold was discovered there, in violation of the treaty's own terms. In *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians* (1980), the U.S. Supreme Court held the seizure unconstitutional and awarded monetary compensation (with accumulated interest); the Sioux Nation has consistently refused to accept the payment, now grown to well over a billion dollars sitting unclaimed in a federal account, insisting on the return of the land itself rather than a monetary settlement — a direct, still-unresolved conflict between a sacred-geography claim and a legal system that can recognize the claim's monetary value but has not returned the land itself. See [[../Americas/North American Indigenous Religions|North American Indigenous Religions]].

## Why pilgrimage matters
Pilgrimage is rarely only one thing. Depending on the tradition and the individual pilgrim, it can be:
- an act of devotion or petition
- penance, or the discharge of a religious obligation
- a marker of identity formation, especially for diaspora communities returning to an ancestral or originary site
- a vehicle for collective memory, reenacting or physically retracing a foundational narrative
- an engine of economic and political integration, historically connecting otherwise disconnected regions through shared travel, trade, and hospitality infrastructure
- a claimed encounter with concentrated sacred power, whether that power is understood to reside permanently in the place or to be periodically renewed through festival and ritual

## Good working summary
Sacred geography is one of religion's strongest material forms. It ties cosmology to land, movement, and memory — and because sacred place is physical and often shared or disputed by more than one tradition, sacred geography is also one of the clearest places where religious claims become entangled with real political stakes: sovereignty, land rights, and the control of access.
