# Africa Overview

## Scope
This section covers religious and spiritual traditions rooted in Africa, along with later transformations through Islam, Christianity, empire, trade, slavery, colonial rule, local continuity, and syncretism.

## Why Africa matters religiously
Africa is not a residual category or a prelude to diaspora religion.
It is one of the foundational religious continents, and one of the most linguistically and culturally diverse — commonly estimated at somewhere between 1,500 and 2,000 living languages, a scale worth keeping in mind whenever "African religion" gets discussed as if it were one thing. This is where you find:
- ancient Egyptian religion and Nile sacred kingship
- diverse indigenous African religious systems across West, Central, Southern, and East Africa
- major West African divine, ancestral, and divinatory worlds
- early and enduring African Christianity
- early and regionally rooted African Islam
- some of the strongest cases of religious continuity through colonial violence and diaspora transformation

A serious library has to treat Africa as primary, not supplementary.

## Major regional clusters
### Nile and Northeast Africa
- ancient Egyptian religion
- sacred kingship, temple systems, funerary religion
- later Christian Ethiopia and Horn of Africa Islam

### West Africa
- divine plurality, ancestor systems, shrine traditions, and powerful divination systems
- Yoruba, Fon/Ewe (Vodun), Akan, Igbo, Dogon, Mande, Mende/Temne, Edo, and many other religious formations — see [[West African Religious Worlds]] and [[West African Deity and Divination Map]]
- major source traditions for Afro-diasporic religion in the Americas

### Central Africa
- Kongo religious cosmology — the *dikenga* cosmogram, *nkisi* power objects, and the *nganga* ritual specialist — one of the largest and, outside specialist scholarship, most underrecognized substrates of Afro-diasporic religion in the Americas; see [[Kongo Religion]]

### Southern and East Africa
- San/Khoisan trance-healing traditions, Zulu and other Nguni ancestor- and diviner-centered religion, Maasai and Nilotic (Nuer, Dinka) traditions — sketched at the regional level in [[Indigenous African Religions]], but not yet built out into dedicated notes at the depth of the West African material above. That gap is worth naming rather than papering over.

### Horn of Africa
- ancient and medieval Christian traditions with deep historical continuity
- Islam as an early, regionally rooted religious force tied to Red Sea exchange and local societies

### African diaspora connections
African traditions do not end at the coast.
Atlantic slavery, colonialism, and displacement transmit and transform African religion into Afro-diasporic forms in the Americas.
That means this shelf links directly with [[../Americas/Overview|Americas]] and, in more depth, [[../Americas/Afro-Diasporic Religions Overview|Afro-Diasporic Religions Overview]].

## Core themes worth tracking
- ancestors, spirits, and layered sacred presence
- sacred kingship and political legitimacy
- divination as an epistemic system, not decorative superstition
- shrine practice, sacred geography, and ritual specialists
- continuity under suppression
- syncretism under coercive historical conditions
- Christian and Islamic adaptation in African contexts rather than simple import models
- how the scholarly category "African Traditional Religion" itself formed, and the live debate (Idowu/Mbiti vs. Okot p'Bitek) over whether it clarifies or flattens what it describes — see [[Indigenous African Religions]]

## Research lenses that help here
- never flatten "African religion" into one system
- distinguish local tradition, regional formation, and diaspora transformation
- watch how divination, ancestors, land, and political order interlock
- treat Christianity and Islam in Africa as African histories, not just foreign overlays
- take colonial suppression and reinterpretation seriously — and read colonial-era missionary and ethnographic sources critically rather than as neutral description, a caution that applies as much to 19th-century accounts of Zulu or Igbo religion as to the better-known cases of Yoruba and Kongo material
- hold emic self-description and etic historical-critical scholarship as explicitly distinct, especially where 20th-century African theologians' own systematizing projects (like "African Traditional Religion") are themselves part of the historical record being studied, not a neutral summary of it

## Recommended resources
- John Mbiti — *African Religions and Philosophy* (1969)
- Okot p'Bitek — *African Religions in Western Scholarship* (1970), the essential counter-reading to Mbiti
- Jacob K. Olupona — *African Religions: A Very Short Introduction*
- J. Lorand Matory — Afro-Atlantic and Yoruba-related work
- Stephan Palmié — Afro-diasporic and Atlantic religious scholarship
- Wyatt MacGaffey — *Religion and Society in Central Africa* (1986), on Kongo religion
- Taddesse Tamrat — Ethiopian church and monarchy
- serious regional ethnography and history over one-size-fits-all summaries

## Good working summary
Africa is a primary civilizational zone of religion, not a footnote. Its traditions are diverse, historically deep, materially grounded, and globally important through both local continuity and diaspora transformation — and the parts of the continent still thin in this library (Southern and East Africa above all) are a limitation of this shelf's coverage, not a reflection of those traditions' actual depth or significance.
