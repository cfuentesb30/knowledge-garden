# Indigenous African Religions

## Scope
This note is a high-level entry point into indigenous African religious systems before collapsing them into one generic category. It is deliberately continental in scope, complementing the regional depth of [[West African Religious Worlds]], [[West African Deity and Divination Map]], and [[Kongo Religion]] rather than repeating their material — this note instead surveys traditions in Southern and East Africa not yet given their own dedicated notes, and works through the historiographic and terminological debates that shape how *any* African tradition gets described in scholarship.

## Why it matters
African religions are often treated as either background folklore or raw material later overwritten by Christianity and Islam.
That is wrong.
These traditions are historically deep, conceptually structured, socially embedded, and often highly resilient under pressure. Africa is also linguistically and culturally the most diverse continent on earth — commonly estimated at somewhere between 1,500 and 2,000 living languages — which is itself a reason the plurality point below is not a rhetorical flourish but a basic demographic fact about the scale of what "African religion" would have to mean if treated as singular.

## Important caution
There is no single "African religion."
Africa contains many religious worlds with different cosmologies, ritual structures, deities, ancestors, divination systems, sacred specialists, political relationships, and local sacred geographies.
A serious researcher starts with plurality, not essence — and stays alert to how easily even well-intentioned scholarship (including some of the African-authored scholarship discussed below) can smuggle a false uniformity back in.

## Common patterns often found
- strong ancestor veneration
- layered spirit worlds
- ritual specialists and diviners
- religion embedded in community, kinship, and land
- less separation between "religious" and "social" life than in many modern categories
- shrine and sacred-place importance
- strong relation between healing, protection, conflict resolution, and sacred mediation

These are recurring *patterns*, not a checklist that every tradition satisfies in the same way or to the same degree — San trance-healing (below) and Yoruba Ifá divination (see [[West African Deity and Divination Map]]) both fit "ritual specialists and diviners" but look almost nothing like each other in practice, structure, or transmission.

## Why divination matters
Many African religious systems do not center authority only in fixed scripture.
They may center it in:
- divination
- elders and ritual specialists
- oral tradition
- embodied performance
- sacred objects and local shrines

That does not make them vague. It makes them structured differently. See [[../Cross-Regional/Scripture vs Oral Tradition|Scripture vs Oral Tradition]] for the comparative argument that oral transmission is a different authority structure, not a weaker one, and [[West African Deity and Divination Map]] for a detailed case study (Ifá) of how sophisticated that structure can be.

## Politics and sacred order
In many indigenous African systems, political authority, lineage order, and sacred order overlap.
Kingship, chieftaincy, ancestor legitimacy, shrine prestige, and ritual authority can be mutually reinforcing — see [[West African Religious Worlds]] for concrete cases (Asante, Benin) and [[../Cross-Regional/Kingship and Divine Legitimacy|Kingship and Divine Legitimacy]] for the comparative frame. This is not a universal African pattern, though: Maasai and San societies below organize religious authority quite differently, with far less centralized political-sacred fusion.

## Regional religious worlds beyond West Africa
West African systems (Yoruba, Fon/Ewe, Akan, and others) are the best-documented in this garden — see the notes linked above. The sketches below exist so the continental picture is not implicitly West Africa plus a footnote.

### Southern Africa: San and Nguni traditions
San-speaking peoples of the Kalahari (the terminology itself is contested: "San" derives from a Khoikhoi term with its own disputed history, and some communities and scholars prefer "Bushmen" as a reclaimed self-designation while others reject it — there is no fully neutral consensus term) maintain one of the longest continuously identifiable rock art traditions in the world, though precise dating and interpretation of specific images remain actively debated among archaeologists. The central living ritual is the **trance** or **healing dance**, documented in detail among the Ju/'hoansi by ethnographers including Lorna Marshall, Richard Katz (*Boiling Energy: Community Healing Among the Kalahari Kung*, 1982), and Megan Biesele. Dancers work to activate **n/om**, described as a potent force based in the body that "boils" up the spine, entering an altered state (**!kia**) used for healing, conflict resolution, and communal wellbeing. Archaeologist David Lewis-Williams has argued that San rock art itself encodes trance experience (the "shamanistic hypothesis") — an influential interpretive framework, but a contested one, not settled fact.

Zulu and other Nguni-speaking traditions of southern Africa center on **uNkulunkulu** ("the very old one"), a remote creator figure associated in origin narratives with a primordial reed bed (**uThlanga**) from which humanity and cattle emerged — structurally similar, though not historically connected, to the "distant high god" pattern found in Olodumare (Yoruba) or Nyame (Akan; see [[West African Deity and Divination Map]]). As in those systems, uNkulunkulu recedes from everyday address. Daily religious life instead centers on the **amadlozi**, ancestral spirits who actively watch over the living (the word derives from *dloza*, "to watch over" or "care for"), reward neglect with illness or misfortune, and are consulted chiefly through **izangoma** (diviner-mediums, sing. *isangoma*, who undergo extended initiation and work through trance) — a distinct specialization from **izinyanga** (herbalist-healers). Axel-Ivar Berglund's *Zulu Thought-Patterns and Symbolism* (1976) is the standard sympathetic, fieldwork-based scholarly account, superseding earlier missionary-produced sources such as Henry Callaway's *The Religious System of the Amazulu* (1868–1870), which read Zulu cosmology heavily through Christian theological categories — the same source-critical caution that applies to colonial-era ethnography of West Africa (see [[West African Deity and Divination Map]]) applies here too.

### East Africa: Maasai and Nilotic traditions
Maasai religion (Kenya and Tanzania) centers on **Enkai** (also Engai or Ngai), a single, androgynous supreme being manifesting in two aspects — *Engai Narok* ("black," linked to rain, fertility, and blessing) and *Engai Na-nyokie* ("red," linked to drought and destructive lightning) — understood not as two gods but as two faces of one. Enkai is addressed directly and continuously; Maasai religious life has no temple and no centralized priesthood, though the **laibon** — a ritual-prophetic specialist who also historically held real political influence, as with the 19th-century laibon Mbatian during the colonial encounter — mediates blessing, prophecy, and healing. Cattle hold specifically religious status: the widely reported belief that Enkai granted all the world's cattle to the Maasai at creation is an emic cosmological claim rather than a historical property record, but it demonstrably shaped how cattle raiding was understood within Maasai communities historically, which is a useful reminder that theological claims can have concrete social consequences independent of their truth-value.

Nilotic traditions of the upper Nile (South Sudan) — Nuer and Dinka religion above all — sit behind some of the most influential, and most methodologically scrutinized, ethnography of African religious thought. E.E. Evans-Pritchard's *Nuer Religion* (1956) described Nuer **Kwoth** ("Spirit," sometimes rendered "God") not as a high god presiding over a tier of lesser deities, and not as a diffuse impersonal force, but as a single spirit manifest simultaneously at multiple levels — sky spirits, totemic spirits, ancestral ghosts — a structure Evans-Pritchard argued resisted easy sorting into either "monotheism" or "polytheism" as those terms are ordinarily used. Godfrey Lienhardt's *Divinity and Experience: The Religion of the Dinka* (1961) made a related argument for neighboring Dinka religion, describing **Powers** (*jok*/*yath*) less as discrete personal deities than as modes through which experience itself — sickness, death, fertility — is apprehended as divine action. Both works remain foundational, and both are still read partly *against* later methodological critique of how much of the "coherent structure" being described was doing analytical work supplied by Evans-Pritchard's own training in Western (including Christian theological) categories rather than emerging cleanly from Nuer or Dinka self-description alone.

### Central Africa
Kongo religious cosmology — organized around the *dikenga* cosmogram, the *nkisi* power object, and the *nganga* ritual specialist, and historically decisive for Afro-diasporic religion in the Americas — is covered in its own dedicated note; see [[Kongo Religion]].

## The "high god" question: a scholarly debate
Wilhelm Schmidt's *Urmonotheismus* ("primordial monotheism") thesis, developed across the multi-volume *Der Ursprung der Gottesidee* (1912–1955), argued that the earliest human societies — reconstructed partly from ethnographic reports on hunter-gatherer groups, including Central African communities documented by Schmidt's student Paul Schebesta — held an original monotheism that later "degenerated" into polytheism through cultural diffusion. The thesis rested on a diffusionist method (*Kulturkreislehre*) now almost universally rejected in anthropology; Evans-Pritchard's own *Theories of Primitive Religion* (1965) is among the most influential critiques, characterizing Schmidt's reconstructions as speculative rather than evidentially grounded.

That episode still casts a shadow over how remote "high god" figures across Africa — Olodumare, Nyame, uNkulunkulu, Enkai, Nuer Kwoth — get discussed today, because two different and still-open questions tend to get collapsed into one. First: did structurally similar remote-creator concepts arise independently across many African societies before sustained colonial contact — a genuine comparative-religion question that stays open. Second, and separately: how much of the *systematic*, doctrinally tidy "African high god" as written up in 20th-century missionary ethnography and later African Christian theology reflects those observers' and theologians' own need to locate something recognizable as monotheism (see the Idowu/Olodumare debate discussed in [[West African Deity and Divination Map]])? Neither question is settled, and treating either as self-evidently resolved is the mistake to avoid.

## "African Traditional Religion" as a category: whose framework?
"African Traditional Religion" (often abbreviated ATR in the scholarly literature) is not a term African communities historically used for their own practice. It is a mid-20th-century academic and theological category, developed substantially by African scholars themselves — E. Bolaji Idowu, John Mbiti, Kwesi Dickson, and others — partly as a corrective project: to have indigenous African religious systems taken seriously as coherent theological traditions on the same footing as Christianity and Islam, rather than dismissed as folklore, "animism" (below), or a "pagan" waystation on the road to conversion.

That project has been influential, and it has also been contested from within African scholarship itself. The Ugandan scholar and poet Okot p'Bitek, in *African Religions in Western Scholarship* (1970), argued that Idowu and especially Mbiti had overcorrected: by systematizing enormously varied local traditions into a single coherent ATR built around an omnipotent, omniscient high god modeled on the Christian deity, they had — in p'Bitek's own words — "smuggled" Greek and Christian metaphysical categories into African material, producing "the African deities of the books": a scholarly construction more legible to Western theology than to what he called "the ordinary Africans in the countryside," whose actual practice he argued was more local, plural, and pragmatic than the ATR synthesis suggested. This is a live methodological disagreement, not a settled matter. Both the impulse behind Idowu and Mbiti's project — taking African religious thought seriously as theology — and p'Bitek's counter-caution — that seriousness can manufacture a false uniformity — are worth holding at the same time, not resolving in advance.

A related terminological problem is the word **"animism."** E.B. Tylor coined it in *Primitive Culture* (1871) to name what he considered the earliest, most primitive stage of religion — the attribution of spirit or soul to natural phenomena — from which he argued more "advanced" religion had evolved. The term rests on an explicitly evolutionist ranking that placed the religions of colonized peoples, African traditions very much included, at the bottom of a ladder culminating, unsurprisingly, in Tylor's own Victorian Protestant Christianity. For that reason this garden avoids using "animism" as a descriptive shorthand for any tradition's engagement with spirit, land, or nonhuman agency; where the underlying phenomenon is worth naming, it is named specifically — ancestor veneration, an ensouled landscape, ritual mediation with nonhuman persons — rather than filed under a term built to rank religions rather than describe them.

## Historical pressures
- Islamization in many regions
- Christian missionization and empire
- colonial suppression, reframing, and pathologizing of local traditions
- survival through adaptation and syncretism
- modern revival, reinterpretation, and contested identity politics

## Why the category is limited
"Indigenous African religions" is useful as a shelf label, but it can hide real differences.
Where possible, study should move from this broad entry note into regional and tradition-specific notes such as [[West African Religious Worlds]], [[West African Deity and Divination Map]], [[Yoruba Religion]], and [[Kongo Religion]] — and, for Southern and East African traditions sketched only briefly above, into the wider comparative and historical literature cited below.

## Research lenses that help
- do not confuse oral tradition with weak tradition
- distinguish ancestor systems, deity systems, and spirit mediation without separating them artificially
- watch how religion, healing, land, and political order interact
- take colonial categories critically; many of them distort what they describe
- keep the Idowu/Mbiti–p'Bitek disagreement in view: systematizing a tradition into recognizable theology and flattening it into false uniformity are different failure modes, and good scholarship has to watch for both

## Recommended resources
- John Mbiti — *African Religions and Philosophy* (1969)
- Okot p'Bitek — *African Religions in Western Scholarship* (1970), the essential counter-reading to Mbiti and Idowu
- Jacob Olupona — African religion studies broadly
- E.E. Evans-Pritchard — *Nuer Religion* (1956) and *Theories of Primitive Religion* (1965)
- Godfrey Lienhardt — *Divinity and Experience: The Religion of the Dinka* (1961)
- Axel-Ivar Berglund — *Zulu Thought-Patterns and Symbolism* (1976)
- Richard Katz — *Boiling Energy: Community Healing Among the Kalahari Kung* (1982)
- regional anthropology and historical studies instead of one-size-fits-all summaries
- scholarship on colonial religious classification and African continuities

## Good working summary
The right starting point for indigenous African religions is diversity, not generalization. These are complex systems of ancestors, spirits, land, ritual specialists, sacred power, and social order with long continuity and major global influence — and the scholarship that describes them, including scholarship written by African theologians defending these traditions' seriousness, is itself a contested, evolving conversation rather than a settled inventory.
