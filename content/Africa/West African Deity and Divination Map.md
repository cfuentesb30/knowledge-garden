# West African Deity and Divination Map

## Purpose
This note gives a reference map for major divine and divinatory patterns across several distinct West African religious systems — chiefly Yoruba, Fon/Ewe (Vodun), and Akan. It is a companion to [[West African Religious Worlds]] (the fuller regional survey) and [[Indigenous African Religions]] (the continental-level framing note). Use it the way you would a deity/pantheon shelf for any other tradition in this garden — as orientation, not as a claim that these are one system wearing different names. Yoruba, Fon, and Akan religion share family-resemblance structures (a distant high god, an active tier of intermediary powers, ancestor veneration, and divination as an epistemic technology) but are historically and linguistically distinct traditions, not regional variants of a single "West African religion."

## A note on sources
Much of the older written record on these traditions comes from colonial-era European ethnography (missionaries, colonial officers, early anthropologists), which frequently imported Christian theological categories (arguing over whether African systems had a "real" high god, treating orisha/vodun/abosom as "fetishes" or "idols," and pathologizing divination and possession as primitive superstition). Contemporary scholarship — Yoruba and Fon/Ewe and Akan scholars writing from within their own academic traditions as much as outside ones — has substantially revised that record. Where older sources are cited below, they are cited critically, not as neutral description.

## Major patterns
- a high god or remote creator principle, generally understood as the ultimate source of existence but not the addressee of everyday ritual
- a populated intermediary tier — orisha (Yoruba), vodun (Fon/Ewe), abosom (Akan) — who receive the bulk of cultic attention because they are approachable, domain-specific, and responsive
- ancestor veneration integrated with, not separate from, the divine tier
- shrine- and priesthood-centered local practice rather than a single central institution or closed canon
- divination as the primary mechanism for diagnosing misfortune, determining ritual obligation, and accessing the will of both ancestors and divine powers
- initiation and embodied practice (possession, masquerade, apprenticeship under a diviner or priest) as the main mode of religious authority and transmission, alongside oral literature

## Yoruba-focused orientation
Yoruba religion (of southwestern Nigeria, Benin, and Togo) is the best-documented of the three traditions mapped here and is the historical source system behind Lucumí/Santería, Candomblé, and other Afro-diasporic traditions; see [[Yoruba Religion]] for the full treatment.

- **Olodumare** (also **Olorun**) — the supreme deity: omniscient, the source of existence, generally too exalted for direct cultic address. Bolaji Idowu's *Olodumare: God in Yoruba Belief* (1962) argued, against earlier colonial dismissals, that Yoruba theology contains a coherent monotheistic-adjacent structure with Olodumare as a genuine supreme being rather than a vague "sky father" abstraction — a position that reframed how scholars treated West African high-god concepts generally, though later scholars (E. Bolaji Idowu's own critics among them) have debated how much of that systematized theology reflects precolonial belief versus 20th-century apologetic synthesis in dialogue with Christianity and Islam.
- **Orisha** — the intermediary divine powers, traditionally numbered "401" (a conventional figure meaning "innumerable" rather than a literal census). Each orisha governs a domain of nature and human experience and has a distinct personality, color, food taboo, and ritual protocol.
  - **Eshu/Elegba** — trickster, messenger, and guardian of crossroads; no Yoruba ritual proceeds without first saluting Eshu, who "opens the road" between human and divine realms
  - **Orunmila** — orisha of wisdom and destiny, patron of Ifá divination (see below)
  - **Ogun** — iron, war, hunting, and the technology of cutting; patron of blacksmiths, hunters, and (in modern devotion) drivers and surgeons
  - **Shango** — thunder and royal justice; in Yoruba oral history, deified as the fourth Alafin (king) of the Oyo Empire, illustrating how sacred kingship and orisha worship interlock
  - **Oshun** — river, fertility, love, and diplomacy; in one widely told narrative she alone succeeds in reconciling humanity with an angered Olodumare, establishing her as indispensable rather than merely decorative
  - **Yemoja** — mother of waters, associated with the ocean and fertility; in the diaspora (as Iemanjá/Yemayá) one of the most widely venerated Afro-Atlantic figures
  - **Obatala** — orisha of creation and purity, credited in Yoruba cosmogony with molding the human form
- **Ase** (*àṣẹ*) — the underlying concept of divine power/authority that all beings possess in some measure; it is what ritual activates, what priests carry in concentrated form, and what makes the orisha system function as power rather than mere symbol.

## Fon/Ewe (Vodun) orientation
Vodun (the source tradition, via the historic kingdom of Dahomey, for Haitian Vodou — see [[../Americas/Vodou|Vodou]]) organizes divinity somewhat differently from the Yoruba model, and scholars caution against reading it as a simple parallel system.
- **Mawu-Lisa** — in Dahomean (Fon) cosmology, often described as a single dual deity fusing Mawu (moon, associated with the west, night, and compassion) and Lisa (sun, associated with the east, day, and strength); the creator principle. Anthropologist Suzanne Preston Blier (*African Vodun: Art, Psychology, and Power*, 1995) and earlier ethnographers note that Vodun communities outside the Dahomean court tradition more often treat Mawu and Lisa as two of several children of an earlier creator (Nana Buluku) rather than as one fused being — a reminder that "the" Fon pantheon is itself court theology generalized outward, not a single fixed system.
- **Legba** — youngest child of Mawu-Lisa in the Dahomean scheme; messenger and gatekeeper between vodun and humans, structurally parallel to Eshu but not identical to him (their iconography, ritual protocol, and mythic role diverge in important ways, even though both are frequently glossed in English as "the trickster/crossroads figure").
- **Sakpata** — vodun of the earth and, notably, of smallpox and skin disease; illness and healing power are directly vested in him rather than treated as a separate category from divine agency.
- **Hevioso/Sogbo** — vodun of thunder and storms.
- Vodun communities in Benin, Togo, and southeastern Ghana use divination (notably **Fa**, cognate with Yoruba Ifá — the systems are historically related, not coincidentally similar) and possession as core epistemic and ritual technologies, structurally parallel to the Yoruba pattern above.

## Akan orientation
Akan religion (Ghana and eastern Côte d'Ivoire — Ashanti, Akuapem, Fante, and related peoples) is organized around a somewhat different divine architecture than either Yoruba or Fon systems.
- **Nyame** (also **Onyankopon**, **Odomankoma**) — the supreme being: creator, omniscient, associated with the sky and with rain; like Olodumare, understood as the ultimate source of existence but distant from routine cultic address.
- **Asase Yaa** — the earth goddess, second in reverence to Nyame; invoked especially around agriculture, burial, and the taking of life from the land (farming, hunting, digging a grave all traditionally require her acknowledgment).
- **Abosom** — the intermediary deities (singular *obosom*), associated with specific rivers, forests, and natural features, worshipped through dedicated priesthoods (*akomfo*) via libation, sacrifice, and possession. Kofi Asare Opoku's *West African Traditional Religion* (1978) remains a standard scholarly entry point written from within Akan/Ghanaian academic religious studies rather than as outside ethnography.
- Akan religious practice places heavy weight on ancestors (*nsamanfo*) and on the stool (*akonnwa*) as a physical seat of both political and spiritual authority — a concrete example of the sacred-kingship overlap discussed in [[West African Religious Worlds]].

## Divination
Divination is not a supplementary or superstitious practice bolted onto these traditions — in all three systems it is the primary technology for accessing sacred knowledge, diagnosing the cause of misfortune, and determining ritual obligation.

**Ifá** (Yoruba) is the most thoroughly documented example. Attributed to the orisha Orunmila, it consists of a corpus of oral literature (**odù**) built from 16 principal signs combined into 256 total configurations, each linked to a large body of memorized verses, proverbs, and prescriptions. A **babalawo** ("father of secrets") casts palm nuts (**ikin**) or a divining chain (**opele**) to generate a configuration, then recites and interprets the associated verses — a process requiring years, often decades, of memorization. Ifá was inscribed by UNESCO in 2005 (and added to the Representative List in 2008) as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. William Bascom's *Ifa Divination: Communication Between Gods and Men in West Africa* (1969) remains a foundational ethnographic study; Wande Abimbola, himself a babalawo and Yoruba scholar, produced the major textual editions of the odù corpus (*Ifa: An Exposition of Ifa Literary Corpus*, 1976).

**Fa** among the Fon is structurally and historically related to Ifá (the two systems share vocabulary, structure, and — per oral tradition and comparative scholarship — likely a common origin), though Fon and Yoruba specialists have each developed distinct interpretive traditions around it.

Among the Akan, divination is practiced by *akomfo* (priests) often through possession-trance rather than a fixed sign-based oracle system comparable to Ifá/Fa, alongside other techniques for reading the will of the abosom and ancestors.

## Why it matters
Treating divination as a side feature — folklore attached to "real" religion — badly misreads all three of these traditions. It is where theology, ethics, medicine, and law converge: a diviner's session addresses not just "what will happen" but why, what ritual or ethical failure caused it, and what must be done. This is structurally comparable to the role scripture and legal interpretation play in text-based traditions, not a lesser or more superstitious analogue to them — the same evidentiary respect due to, say, halakhic or hadith reasoning is due here.
