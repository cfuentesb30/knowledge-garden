# Andean Religions Overview

## Scope
This note surveys major religious patterns of the Andean world across roughly three millennia — pre-Inca ceremonial civilizations, Inca imperial state religion, local sacred geography and agricultural reciprocity, and the colonial Christian campaigns and Andean responses that followed the Spanish conquest (1532). It is a companion to [[Mesoamerican Religions Overview]] and [[Mesoamerican Deity and Calendar Map]]: read together, the two shelves make clear that "pre-Columbian religion" is not one thing, even within a single continent — Andean and Mesoamerican systems developed independently, on different timelines, with different core logics (landscape/reciprocity here; calendar/cosmic-debt there).

## Why it matters
Andean religion matters because it is one of the clearest cases in world religion of:
- sacred geography as active reality, not metaphor
- mountain and landscape power
- reciprocity between humans, land, ancestors, and sacred beings as an organizing ethical logic, not just a ritual gesture
- state ritual layered over a deep substrate of local cults that predate, and outlasted, any single empire
- colonial Christianity interacting with, suppressing, and blending with older systems — and, in places, meeting organized religious resistance

## Important caution
"Andean religion" is not one single unified creed.
The Andes contain a sequence of major cultures spanning roughly 3,000 years before, during, and beyond the Inca — Chavín, Moche, Nazca, Tiwanaku, Wari, and Chimú among them (below) — each with its own religious system, only some of which is well understood archaeologically. The Inca become politically dominant in the century and a half before Spanish contact, but they are not metaphysically exhaustive: local religious life, including cults many centuries older than the Inca state, remains crucial and in many respects continues into the present day.

## Pre-Inca religious horizons
Before Inca imperial religion (below), the Andes produced several major, independently developed religious traditions, and the archaeological and art-historical record for them is central to understanding what the Inca later absorbed and reorganized rather than invented from nothing.

- **Chavín** (centered on the site of Chavín de Huántar, highland Peru, flourishing roughly 1200–500 BCE) is widely treated as the earliest major pan-Andean religious horizon: its distinctive art style — fanged, composite feline/serpent/raptor imagery, most famously carved on the **Lanzón**, a monolith functioning as an oracle deep inside the temple's internal gallery system, and the **Tello Obelisk** — spread across a wide area of coastal and highland Peru not through conquest but, per Richard Burger's influential account (*Chavín and the Origins of Andean Civilization*, 1992) and subsequent scholarship, through pilgrimage and the prestige of a shared religious complex.
- **Moche** (north coast, roughly 100–800 CE) left an unusually explicit iconographic record of its central cult, organized around a deity commonly called **Ai Apaec** ("the Decapitator" or "the Maker") — depicted with fangs, feline and serpent attributes, and a severed human head or ceremonial blade (*tumi*) in hand. The so-called "Sacrifice Ceremony," long known only from painted pottery, was corroborated archaeologically by the 1987 discovery of the royal tombs at **Sipán**, which contained the same ceremonial goblets and regalia depicted in the ceramic scenes — a rare case of Andean religious iconography and mortuary archaeology confirming each other directly.
- **Nazca** (south coast, roughly 100 BCE–800 CE) produced the famous Nazca Lines — enormous geoglyphs of animals, plants, and geometric figures visible fully only from the air. Their purpose is genuinely contested rather than settled: Maria Reiche's mid-20th-century astronomical-calendar hypothesis (that the lines track solstices and star risings) was highly influential but is now regarded by most specialists as insufficient on its own; later work (including Johan Reinhard's and other archaeologists' field studies) has argued the lines functioned at least partly as ritual pathways connected to water and fertility rites, walked rather than only viewed. No single explanation commands full consensus.
- **Tiwanaku** (Lake Titicaca basin, flourishing roughly 500–1000 CE) and the roughly contemporaneous **Wari** state were major highland religious and political centers whose monumental architecture (including Tiwanaku's carved **Gateway of the Sun**) and iconography influenced later Inca sacred architecture and imperial self-understanding, including Inca origin narratives that root creation itself at Lake Titicaca (see Viracocha, below).
- **Chimú** (north coast, centered on the city of Chan Chan, roughly 900–1470 CE, conquered by the Inca shortly before Spanish contact) preserved its own dynastic and religious traditions, including the founding myth of the culture-hero **Naylamp**, arriving by sea to establish sacred kingship — a narrative pattern of divinely sanctioned founding migration comparable in function, though not content, to Mexica or Maya dynastic myth (see [[Mesoamerican Deity and Calendar Map]]).

## Important divine beings and sacred powers
- **Inti** — the sun deity of major importance in Inca state religion, closely tied to the person of the Sapa Inca (the emperor), who was understood as Inti's descendant; the state cult of Inti, and its associated festival Inti Raymi (below), appear to have been substantially elaborated under the emperor Pachacuti (r. mid-15th century) as part of a broader systematization of Inca state religion during the empire's rapid expansion, rather than representing an unchanging timeless practice.
- **Viracocha** (also Con Tici Viracocha) — a creator deity recorded in chronicles including Juan de Betanzos's account (mid-16th century): rising from Lake Titicaca in an age of darkness, Viracocha creates the sun, moon, and stars, forms and then destroys a first race of stone giants in a great flood, creates humanity anew, teaches people language, agriculture, and the arts, and finally departs westward across the Pacific, promising to return. As with Quetzalcoatl in Mexica tradition (see [[Mesoamerican Deity and Calendar Map]]), a "departing god who promises return" narrative recorded through colonial-era chroniclers should be treated with the same caution: how much reflects genuine pre-contact belief versus post-conquest elaboration (in some cases shaped by the same colonial-era narrative pattern used to explain Spanish arrival as a fulfilled prophecy) is a live source-critical question, not a settled fact.
- **Pachamama** — earth/fertility power, prominent in Andean religious language recorded from the colonial period onward and very much alive in contemporary Andean devotion; scholars caution against treating "Pachamama" as a simple, unchanging pre-Inca figure — the term and cult as documented may owe something to the long colonial and postcolonial period of Catholic-Andean interaction, alongside older substrates.
- **Pachacamac** — a distinct major coastal creator/oracle deity, with an important pilgrimage temple-complex of the same name near modern Lima that predates Inca control and was incorporated, rather than replaced, when the Inca absorbed the coast — an example of the layering-not-replacement pattern discussed below. Pachacamac and Pachamama are related in name and domain but are not the same figure, and older popular sources sometimes conflate them.
- **apus** — mountain spirits or powers, understood across many Andean communities, historically and in the present, as living, personal, and locally specific (a named apu belongs to a particular peak and community) rather than as generic nature-worship.
- **huacas** — a broader category than named deities: any sacred place, object, ancestor-mummy, or natural feature understood to hold sacred power. Inca Cusco organized 328 recognized huacas around the capital along 41 conceptual lines radiating from the Coricancha (the Temple of the Sun) — the **ceque system**, analyzed in the foundational structuralist study by R. Tom Zuidema (*The Ceque System of Cuzco*, 1964), who argued the system encoded calendrical, astronomical, and social-organizational information simultaneously, tying sacred geography directly to timekeeping and social hierarchy rather than treating them as separate domains.

## Ritual life
- offerings to land and sacred places, and reciprocal exchange (*ayni*) with land and sacred powers rather than one-way worship alone
- calendrical and agricultural rites, closely bound to the Inca state's own solar and lunar calendrical apparatus
- ancestor-linked practices, including the preservation and periodic ritual attention of royal mummies (*mallki*), who continued to hold property and receive offerings after death
- state ritual in imperial contexts, including **Inti Raymi**, the June solstice festival honoring Inti, associated in some chronicle traditions with Pachacuti's reforms and revived in the 20th century as a major public festival in Cusco
- the **capacocha**: a ritual, documented both in colonial chronicles and archaeologically, in which children and young people — selected, per the sources, partly for physical perfection and partly as tribute from provincial communities, and regarded within Inca ideology as a high honor rather than a punishment — were brought to Cusco, ritually processed, and in some cases sacrificed at high-altitude shrines, often mountain summits, typically in response to major state events (an imperial accession, a natural disaster) rather than as routine practice. Frozen mummies recovered from sites including Llullaillaco (Argentina) have allowed direct biomolecular study: hair isotope analysis (Andrew Wilson and colleagues, published in *PNAS*, 2013) shows a marked increase in coca-leaf and maize-beer (*chicha*) consumption in the final months of at least one child's life, consistent with chronicled accounts and suggesting sedation in the period leading up to death — a rare instance where colonial-era textual description and modern forensic archaeology substantially corroborate each other.
- sacred landscape pilgrimage and localized devotion, discussed further below

## State and local religion
This distinction matters.
Inca imperial religion created broader political-sacred organization — the Inti cult, the ceque system, capacocha, and a centralized priesthood based at Coricancha — but local sacred life, including cults that predate the Inca by centuries (Pachacamac chief among them), remained strong and was in most documented cases incorporated rather than erased. The relation between empire and local cult was one of layering and incorporation, not total replacement — a pattern with a rough structural parallel, though a different specific logic, in how Mexica state religion absorbed older Mesoamerican deities like Tlaloc (see [[Mesoamerican Deity and Calendar Map]]).

## Historical patterns
### Pre-Inca and Inca layers
See "Pre-Inca religious horizons" above. The Inca system becomes politically dominant across a vast territory only in the roughly 100 years before Spanish contact, a much shorter span than the deep history of Andean religion as a whole — worth remembering whenever "Inca religion" gets used as a stand-in for "Andean religion."

### Colonial transformation and Andean resistance
Catholicism overlays, suppresses, and blends with Andean practice in complex and regionally varied ways, producing strong cases of syncretism and continuity under new symbols, feasts, saints, and shrine structures (see [[../Cross-Regional/Syncretism Overview|Syncretism Overview]]). Two specific episodes make this concrete rather than abstract:

- **The extirpation of idolatry campaigns** (concentrated roughly 1570s–1660s) were organized ecclesiastical inquiries, closely paralleling the Inquisition in method though institutionally distinct from it, aimed at locating and destroying huacas, mummies, and ritual objects and prosecuting practitioners. Jesuit priest Pablo José de Arriaga's *La extirpación de la idolatría en el Perú* (1621) is the best-known surviving manual from the campaigns — an invaluable but explicitly missionary source, written as a field guide for suppression, and one that (like other colonial-era ethnographic sources treated elsewhere in this garden) needs to be read for what it reveals about Andean practice *and* for its own agenda. Modern critical scholarship on the campaigns, notably Kenneth Mills's *Idolatry and Its Enemies* (1997), works from and against these sources rather than taking them at face value.
- One product of this same campaign is, paradoxically, one of the richest surviving windows into Andean religious thought in something close to its own terms: the **Huarochirí Manuscript**, compiled in Quechua around 1608, likely at the instigation of Francisco de Ávila — himself a leading extirpator — yet preserving myth, ritual description, and cosmology substantially in Andean categories rather than through a purely external missionary lens. Frank Salomon and George Urioste's *The Huarochirí Manuscript* (1991) is the standard English translation and remains a foundational primary source, comparable in importance to the Popol Vuh for Mesoamerica (see [[Mesoamerican Deity and Calendar Map]]).
- **Taki Onqoy** ("dancing sickness"), documented from around 1564–1572 principally through the writings of the same Cristóbal de Albornoz who worked to suppress it, was a religious revitalization movement in the central highlands holding that the huacas — understood as neglected and damaged, not destroyed, by Spanish conquest and Christian suppression — were returning in force to defeat the Christian God and expel Spanish rule, and calling on Andean communities to reject Spanish food, dress, and the Christian faith. Its scale is itself contested: some later scholarship, working from archaeological evidence in specific valleys (Chicha-Soras), estimates participation at roughly 8,000 of a regional population of 150,000 — suggesting a real but regionally uneven movement rather than the fully coordinated pan-Andean uprising that Albornoz's own alarmed reports (written to justify his own campaign) sometimes imply. Historian Sabine MacCormack's work is a standard reference for reading this and related episodes critically.

## Andean religion as a living tradition
It is a mistake to treat Andean religion as something that ended with the conquest. Catherine Allen's ethnography of the Quechua-speaking community of Sonqo, *The Hold Life Has: Coca and Cultural Identity in an Andean Community* (1988, 2nd ed. 2002), documents a living, reciprocal relationship with apus, Pachamama, and coca as sacred substance that continues in the present, structured by the same underlying logic of *ayni* (reciprocity) discussed throughout this note rather than as a survival curiosity. **Qoyllur Rit'i**, an annual pilgrimage to a glacier sanctuary in the Cusco region combining devotion to an image of Christ with apu veneration and ritual roles reserved for *ukukus* (dancers embodying a bear-spirit figure who traditionally ascend the glacier itself), is one of the largest Indigenous pilgrimages in South America and a clear present-day case of the layering pattern described throughout this note; see [[../Cross-Regional/Sacred Geography and Pilgrimage|Sacred Geography and Pilgrimage]].

## Why it matters comparatively
Andean religion is especially useful for studying:
- sacred landscape as an organizing religious category in its own right, not just a backdrop
- reciprocity as religious logic
- mountain power, oracle shrines, and huaca networks
- colonial layering and religious resistance movements rather than simple, total replacement
- how a religious tradition can be simultaneously ancient, imperially systematized, violently suppressed, and still actively practiced today

## Good working summary
Andean religions are landscape-centered, reciprocal, agricultural, ancestral, and highly resilient — spanning millennia of pre-Inca ceremonial civilizations, a relatively brief but highly organized Inca imperial synthesis, violent colonial suppression that was also met with organized religious resistance, and a living present-day practice. To understand them, you have to read mountains, offerings, empire, extirpation, and living continuity together, not treat any one period as the whole story.
