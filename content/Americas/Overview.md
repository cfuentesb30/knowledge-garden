# Americas Overview

## Scope
This section covers Indigenous religious worlds of the Americas, Mesoamerican and Andean traditions, North American Indigenous religion, colonial transformation, Catholic layering, African diasporic formation, and the continuing reshaping of sacred life across the hemisphere.

## Why the Americas matter religiously
The Americas are not only a site of conquest and replacement.
They are one of the strongest laboratories of religious collision, survival, and recombination, spanning independently developed civilizations that in some cases (Andean Chavín and Mesoamerican Olmec religious traditions, for instance) predate the Common Era by well over a thousand years. This shelf matters for understanding:
- Indigenous cosmology and sacred geography across hundreds of distinct nations and peoples, not a single "Native American" template
- state religion in Mesoamerican and Andean systems
- colonial Christianity as both imposition and adaptation
- Afro-diasporic religion as transformed continuity, produced by a slave trade whose scale and regional structure directly shaped which African traditions ended up where
- syncretism under violence, and the live scholarly debate over whether "syncretism" is even the right word for what happened
- the survival of land-, ancestor-, and ritual-based systems inside hostile orders — including systems still actively practiced today, not only historical artifacts

## Major regional clusters
### Mesoamerica
Highly structured calendrical, cosmological, temple-centered, and politically integrated religions among the Maya (a language family and cultural sphere spanning roughly three millennia) and the Mexica/Aztec (a specific, much later imperial culture), and related traditions — see [[Mesoamerican Religions Overview]] and [[Mesoamerican Deity and Calendar Map]].

### Andes
Sacred geography, mountain power, reciprocity, agricultural ritual — spanning pre-Inca civilizations (Chavín, Moche, Nazca, Tiwanaku, Chimú) as well as the much shorter-lived Inca imperial synthesis, colonial suppression met with organized religious resistance, and a living present-day practice — see [[Andean Religions Overview]].

### North American Indigenous traditions
Hundreds of distinct Indigenous nations across North America — Haudenosaunee, Lakota, Diné, Anishinaabe, Pueblo peoples, Tlingit, Inuit, and hundreds of others — each with its own cosmology, ceremonial system, and relationship to land; see [[North American Indigenous Religions]] for the structural patterns (land-based cosmology, relational ontology, ceremony as cosmic maintenance) that recur across this diversity, the specific colonial disruption these traditions faced (residential/boarding schools, the U.S. Indian Religious Crimes Code of 1883–1978), and the analytical principles for studying them responsibly. Given the scale of the field — hundreds of independent traditions — this remains, by design, a broad structural map rather than an exhaustive regional survey, with room for future tradition-specific notes.

### Afro-diasporic Americas
Religious systems formed under slavery, displacement, and colonial domination through transformed continuity with African source traditions — Vodou, Santería/Lucumí, Candomblé, Abakuá, Obeah/Myal, Garifuna religion, and Rastafarianism among them, each shaped differently by which African region, which colonial power, and (for the Garifuna) which Indigenous population were in the mix. See [[Afro-Diasporic Religions Overview]], [[Vodou]], [[Santería and Lucumí]], [[Candomblé]], and [[Rastafarianism]].

### Colonial and postcolonial Christian interaction
Catholicism becomes historically central, but never simply replaces earlier systems cleanly. Layering, disguise, adaptation, and conflict matter — including organized Indigenous religious resistance to Christian suppression, such as the Andean Taki Onqoy movement of the 1560s (see [[Andean Religions Overview]]).

## Core themes worth tracking
- sacred geography and land relation
- calendrical ritual and cosmic maintenance
- sacrifice, offering, and reciprocal obligation
- colonial suppression and religious masking — and, in places, direct religious resistance rather than only accommodation
- syncretism under power asymmetry, and whether that term itself flattens real internal distinctions traditions maintain
- ritual continuity through diaspora and reinvention
- the demographic and historical specificity behind "African religion in the Americas" — which African region, which period of the slave trade, which colonial power, shaped genuinely different outcomes rather than one generic process

## Research lenses that help here
- do not reduce Indigenous religion to sacrifice or folklore
- take sacred landscape seriously
- treat colonial Christianity as historically powerful but never total
- distinguish continuity, adaptation, disguise, and invention
- keep Atlantic slavery and African transmission in view when reading the Americas shelf — and keep the specific regional and demographic structure of that transmission in view too, rather than treating "African religion arrived in the Americas" as a single undifferentiated event
- treat "Native American religion" and "Afro-diasporic religion" with the same specificity expected of any other tradition in this garden — naming the particular nation, people, or tradition rather than defaulting to the broad category

## Recommended resources
- Davíd Carrasco — *Religions of Mesoamerica*
- Alfredo López Austin — Mesoamerican religion and thought
- Sabine MacCormack — *Religion in the Andes: Vision and Imagination in Early Colonial Peru* (1991)
- Gary Urton / Catherine Allen / R. Tom Zuidema for Andean worlds
- Vine Deloria Jr. — *God Is Red: A Native View of Religion*, for North American Indigenous religion from inside the tradition
- J. Lorand Matory / Stephan Palmié for Afro-Atlantic religion
- Melville Herskovits and E. Franklin Frazier, read against each other, for the foundational debate over African cultural survival in the Americas
- serious regional historical scholarship over flattening survey language

## Good working summary
The Americas are a religious zone of collision, continuity, and transformation. Indigenous sacred worlds — Mesoamerican, Andean, and North American, each internally as diverse as any other regional cluster in this garden — colonial Christianity, and African diasporic traditions meet here under unequal power, producing some of the most complex religious layering in the world, much of it still actively practiced rather than only historical.
