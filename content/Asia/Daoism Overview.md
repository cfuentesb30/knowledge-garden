# Daoism Overview

## Scope
Daoism (Taoism) includes both philosophical and religious traditions centered on the Dao, naturalness, ritual order, body-spirit cultivation, sacred bureaucracy, immortality, and the alignment of human life with cosmic process. It spans roughly 2,500 years, from the classical texts attributed to Laozi and Zhuangzi through organized priestly lineages that still ordain clergy and run temples today in China, Taiwan, and the diaspora.

## Why it matters
Daoism is often mangled in modern summaries into a few detached quotes about effortless living, sold as a lifestyle philosophy stripped of institutions, clergy, and cosmology.
That is far too small, and it is also not symmetrical with how other traditions in this library are treated — nobody summarizes Christianity as a set of inspirational quotations detached from church, priesthood, and sacrament.
Daoism includes:
- philosophical reflection
- ritual religion, with ordained priests performing liturgy for hire and for community festivals
- priesthood and temples organized into distinct lineages, some celibate and monastic, some hereditary and married
- cosmology and a bureaucratic model of the divine world
- healing and exorcistic work
- alchemical and longevity practices, both chemical/external and meditative/internal
- interaction with state power, Confucian institutions, Buddhism, and folk religion across every dynasty

## The daojia/daojiao question
Modern scholarship of Daoism, especially in English-language textbooks, has long organized the tradition into two boxes: **daojia** ("philosophical Daoism" — Laozi, Zhuangzi, the classical texts) and **daojiao** ("religious Daoism" — the organized priesthood, liturgy, and cosmology that develops from the Han dynasty onward). This split is useful for a first orientation, but specialists increasingly treat it as a distortion rather than a discovery.

Kristofer Schipper, one of the most influential Western scholars of Daoism (himself an ordained Zhengyi priest), has argued that no historical Daoist canon — including the Ming-dynasty *Daozang* of 1445 — ever separated *daojia* and *daojiao* into different categories; in the primary sources the two terms function as near-synonyms for "the lineage/house of the Dao" and "the teachings of the Dao." The philosophy/religion split reflects a 20th-century Western and Republican-era Chinese reformist assumption — that "true" philosophy is rational and textual while "mere religion" is ritual and superstitious — projected back onto material that did not originally carry that distinction. Russell Kirkland and Isabelle Robinet have made related arguments: that treating the *Daodejing* as pure philosophy severed from the later ritual tradition, or treating religious Daoism as a corruption/decline from an original philosophical purity, both misread the historical record. This is a live methodological disagreement, not a settled matter, and it is worth naming explicitly rather than silently reproducing the philosophy/religion split as if it were simply how Daoism is organized.

## Textual anchors
### Early philosophical texts
- **Daodejing** (*Tao Te Ching*), traditionally attributed to **Laozi** ("Old Master"). Current Western scholarship is close to unanimous that there was no single historical author matching the traditional biography (a 6th-century-BCE contemporary of Confucius who supposedly transmitted the text to a border guard before riding west). Excavated manuscripts — the Guodian bamboo-slip version (c. 300 BCE, a partial and differently-ordered text) and the Mawangdui silk manuscripts (2nd century BCE) — show the text existing in multiple, evolving forms before any single "original" was fixed, consistent with the view that "Laozi" names a composite tradition and its emblematic teacher-figure rather than one identifiable person.
- **Zhuangzi**, attributed to a figure of the same name (traditionally 4th century BCE) but understood by scholars as a composite text: the 7 "Inner Chapters" are generally treated as the oldest and most coherent stratum, while the "Outer" and "Miscellaneous" chapters reflect later, plural authorship and sometimes divergent philosophical positions, compiled over subsequent centuries.

These texts become crucial reference points for the whole tradition, but Daoism is larger than them, and later religious Daoism does not treat them as a closed philosophical canon so much as scripture to be ritually recited, commented on, and drawn into liturgy.

### Religious canons and ritual texts
Later Daoist traditions generate extensive textual corpora, liturgies, registers, talismanic systems, and ritual manuals, collected into the **Daozang** (Daoist Canon). The idea of a Daoist textual repository goes back to at least the Han dynasty; the canon was repeatedly compiled, expanded, and reprinted — a Song-dynasty printed edition (the *Zhenghe Wanshou Daozang*, completed 1119) used roughly 70,000 printing blocks — and the version still used today, the **Zhengtong Daozang**, was compiled under Ming-dynasty patronage and completed in 1445, containing over 1,400 texts organized into three "caverns" (*dong*) reflecting different revelatory lineages (Shangqing, Lingbao, and the older Sanhuang material) plus supplementary sections.

## Core concepts
### Dao
The Dao is the Way: source, process, order, and generative pattern that exceeds rigid definition.
It is not a simple object or deity, and the opening line of the *Daodejing* ("the Dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao") is itself a warning against treating the concept as a fixed doctrine.

### Wu wei
Often translated as non-forcing, non-action, or effortless action.
This does not mean laziness or passivity. It points to alignment with process rather than clumsy, coercive domination of it — action so well-fitted to circumstance that it appears effortless, a recurring political ideal as much as a personal one (the sage-ruler who governs without visible interference).

### Ziran and De
**Ziran** ("self-so," "spontaneity," "naturalness") describes how things unfold when not distorted by artificial imposition — not "nature" in the biological sense but the condition of being generated from one's own inner pattern rather than external coercion. **De** ("virtue," "power," "efficacy") is the Dao's presence and operative power as it manifests in a particular person, thing, or situation — related to but distinct from the Confucian *de*, which is more explicitly moral.

### Harmony and transformation
Daoist thought and practice often emphasize balance, flow, adaptation, cultivation, and alignment with a wider cosmic order, expressed through the interplay of yin and yang and the five phases (*wuxing*) — a correlative cosmology Daoism shares with, and helped shape alongside, [[Chinese Folk Religion]] and traditional Chinese medicine.

## Religious Daoism
Religious Daoism includes more than philosophy.
It can involve:
- ordained priests and ritual specialists, trained in specific lineages with distinct qualifications and liturgical repertoires
- temple systems, both Daoist-staffed temples and the broader landscape of local shrines described in [[Chinese Folk Religion]]
- a celestial bureaucracy modeled on (and historically entangled with) the imperial civil administration — see [[Chinese Folk Religion]] for how this bureaucratic cosmology plays out at the popular level
- immortals and deified human figures
- talismans (*fu*) and registers (*lu*) — sacred documents that grant a priest ritual authority over specific spirits and deities, transmitted through initiation
- liturgy, including large-scale communal rites (*jiao*) of cosmic renewal performed for entire communities
- healing, protection, and exorcistic practice
- inner alchemy (*neidan*) and body cultivation, aimed at transforming the practitioner's own vital essence, breath, and spirit (*jing*, *qi*, *shen*) into a purified, immortal body — as distinct from earlier "external alchemy" (*waidan*), the laboratory compounding of elixirs from minerals and metals, which carried real physical risk (a number of Tang emperors are recorded as having died from elixir poisoning)

## Historical development
Daoism develops over many centuries in interaction with Chinese state formation, local cults and folk religion, Buddhism, Confucian traditions, and changing imperial conditions. It is not a timeless bubble; specific lineages emerge at datable moments, in competition and negotiation with rival institutions.

### The Way of the Celestial Masters (from 142 CE)
The first organized religious Daoist movement. Tradition holds that in 142 CE **Zhang Daoling** received a revelation on Mount Heming (in present-day Sichuan) from the deified Laozi (Taishang Laojun), who bestowed on him the title "Celestial Master" (*tianshi*) and a covenant establishing a new religious order. The movement — initially nicknamed the "Way of Five Pecks of Rice" for the grain due paid by members — built a theocratic regional community with its own moral code, healing rites, and register system, briefly achieving political independence in Sichuan before submitting to Han authority. The hereditary Celestial Master lineage claims unbroken descent from Zhang Daoling and continues today, based in Taiwan since 1949.

### Shangqing and Lingbao revelations (4th–5th centuries)
The **Shangqing** ("Highest Clarity") tradition emerged from visionary revelations received by Yang Xi at Mao Shan (Maoshan) between 364 and 370 CE, emphasizing individual meditative visualization, celestial travel, and refined textual transmission to an elite adept rather than a mass congregation. The **Lingbao** ("Numinous Treasure") tradition followed in the early 5th century, synthesizing Celestial Masters ritual structure with Mahayana Buddhist ideas of universal salvation, producing large-scale communal liturgies aimed at saving all beings rather than only the initiated adept — an early, concrete instance of Daoist–Buddhist doctrinal cross-pollination rather than one-way borrowing.

### Tang patronage and imperial politics
The Tang imperial house (618–907), surnamed Li, claimed descent from Laozi (whose personal name was traditionally given as Li Er) and used this genealogy to legitimate dynastic rule, giving Daoism a period of substantial state patronage, examination-system status for Daoist texts, and monumental temple building — alongside recurring court rivalry with Buddhist institutions for imperial favor.

### Quanzhen and Zhengyi: the two surviving major branches
Under the Jin dynasty in north China, **Wang Chongyang** (1113–1170) founded the **Quanzhen** ("Complete Perfection") order: a celibate, monastic form of Daoism emphasizing internal alchemy, meditation, and moral discipline, organizationally influenced by the Chan Buddhist monastery model it competed with. Quanzhen became dominant in northern China; its principal modern center is the Baiyunguan (White Cloud Temple) in Beijing. In the south, the older **Zhengyi** ("Orthodox Unity") tradition — the continuation of the Celestial Masters lineage — remained a married, hereditary priesthood serving lay communities through ritual performance rather than monastic withdrawal. These are the only two Daoist ordination lineages still officially recognized in China today; smaller regional and Taiwanese lineages (including Zhengyi variants) also persist.

### Suppression and revival
The Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) destroyed or repurposed a large proportion of Daoist temples, defrocked or persecuted clergy, and banned public ritual practice, paralleling the treatment of [[Chinese Folk Religion]] and other traditions in the same period. Since the 1980s, state-registered Daoist associations, temple reconstruction, and priestly ordination have revived substantially, alongside continuous, uninterrupted Daoist institutional life in Taiwan and among diaspora communities throughout the 20th century.

## Why it matters comparatively
Daoism is an excellent case for understanding:
- the overlap — and contested boundary — between philosophy and religion as etic scholarly categories
- sacred bureaucracy and cosmology as an organizing metaphor for the divine world
- body cultivation and ritual religion coexisting in one system rather than being alternatives
- coexistence rather than clean exclusivism among traditions, alongside [[Confucian Tradition]] and [[Buddhism Overview]] within the same civilizational space — see [[../Cross-Regional/Syncretism Overview|Syncretism Overview]]

## Good working summary
Daoism is a wide civilizational complex of philosophy, ritual, cosmology, priesthood, body cultivation, and sacred practice, organized across identifiable historical lineages (Celestial Masters, Shangqing, Lingbao, Quanzhen, Zhengyi) rather than a single timeless teaching. The biggest mistake is reducing it to inspirational minimalism; a close second is treating the philosophy/religion split as a neutral description rather than a contested scholarly frame.
