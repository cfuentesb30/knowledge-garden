# Shinto Overview

## Scope
Shinto is the indigenous religious tradition of Japan, centered on kami, ritual purity, shrine networks, sacred landscape, seasonal festivals, imperial myth, and local continuity. As discussed below, the word "Shinto" and the idea of a single, unified tradition by that name are themselves the product of specific historical moments — the tradition's deep roots in Japanese local practice and its identity as one bounded religion are not the same claim, and conflating them is one of the most common ways Shinto gets misdescribed.

## Why it matters
Shinto matters because it shows how religion can be rooted more in:
- place
- purity
- seasonal ritual
- shrine practice
- communal continuity
- sacred presence in landscape and lineage

than in one fixed creed or one canonical theology — while also being one of the clearest cases in world religion of a modern state actively reshaping, centralizing, and at times inventing tradition under the label of restoring an ancient one. Both things are true at once, and a serious treatment holds them together rather than picking the folkloric version or the state-history version and ignoring the other. Shinto deserves the same specificity and source-critical scrutiny routinely given to, say, the formation of the biblical canon (see [[../Middle East/Ancient Israelite Religion|Ancient Israelite Religion]]) — treating it as vaguer or more "exotic" than text-based Abrahamic traditions is itself a bias worth naming.

## Core concept: kami
Kami are often translated as gods, spirits, presences, or sacred powers, but no single English word works cleanly.
Kami may be:
- associated with mountains, rivers, storms, fertility, ancestors, rulers, and local places
- morally mixed or situational rather than simple good/evil beings
- local, regional, or nationally central

Traditional reckoning speaks of *yaoyorozu no kami*, "eight million kami" — a way of saying the number is uncountable rather than a literal census. The 18th-century nativist scholar **Motoori Norinaga**, in his study of the *Kojiki*, gave one of the most influential native (emic) definitions: kami are anything that inspires a sense of awe, reverence, or extraordinary power — from great deities to a striking rock, tree, or animal — a definition deliberately broader and less anthropomorphic than "god" implies in Abrahamic usage.

## Important kami figures
- **Amaterasu** — sun kami, central in imperial mythic legitimacy; the imperial line is traditionally held to descend from her, and the Ise Grand Shrine (Ise Jingu) is her principal shrine
- **Susanoo** — storm, turbulence, unruly power, but not merely villainy — exiled from heaven for disruptive conduct, he later becomes a culture hero who slays a monstrous serpent and produces a sacred sword
- **Tsukuyomi** — moon-associated figure in the broader mythic pattern, comparatively undeveloped in surviving myth relative to his siblings Amaterasu and Susanoo
- **Inari** — kami of rice, agriculture, fertility, and (in a distinct later strand) prosperity and trade; among the most numerous shrine dedications in Japan, with foxes (*kitsune*) as messengers/attendants
- numerous local shrine kami (*ujigami*, tutelary/clan kami) with strong regional identity, often specific to a single community or lineage

These figures are recorded primarily in Japan's two oldest surviving chronicles, the **Kojiki** (712 CE) and the **Nihon Shoki** (720 CE), both compiled under Yamato court sponsorship in the Nara period. Both texts are explicit political documents as much as religious ones: their central organizing concern is establishing the divine descent and unbroken legitimacy of the imperial line, and the Nihon Shoki in particular was composed using Chinese historiographical conventions to present Japan's court as a legitimate peer to Chinese dynastic tradition. That does not make the mythic material fabricated wholesale — much of it plausibly draws on older oral and regional tradition — but it means these texts should be read as court-shaped compilations with a legitimating purpose, the same critical stance this library takes toward comparable royal-mythic literature elsewhere (see [[../Cross-Regional/Kingship and Divine Legitimacy|Kingship and Divine Legitimacy]]).

## Ritual life
Shinto is highly ritual and shrine-centered.
Common features include:
- purification (*harae*, removing pollution/impurity, and *misogi*, purification by water) — see [[../Cross-Regional/Purity and Pollution Systems|Purity and Pollution Systems]]
- shrine visits, including a standard approach sequence (purification at a water basin, bowing, offering, clapping, prayer)
- offerings of food, sake, and other goods
- prayer petitions, often for specific practical outcomes (health, safe childbirth, exam success, business prosperity) rather than only devotional worship
- festivals (*matsuri*), ranging from small local shrine observances to major citywide events like the Gion Matsuri in Kyoto; matsuri typically combine ritual (kami invited into a portable shrine, or *mikoshi*, and processed through the community), entertainment, and economic activity — see [[../Cross-Regional/Festivals and Sacred Time|Festivals and Sacred Time]]
- seasonal observance tied to the agricultural and lunar-solar calendar
- local shrine-community ties, historically organized around parishioner households (*ujiko*) tied to a given shrine's *ujigami*

## Sacred space and objects
Shinto places strong emphasis on:
- shrine space (*jinja*), typically including an outer and inner precinct
- the **torii** gate, marking the boundary between ordinary and sacred space — one of the most recognizable visual markers of Shinto
- **shimenawa** (sacred straw rope, often hung with *shide* paper streamers), used to mark and cordon sacred objects, trees, rocks, or boundaries
- a **shintai** ("kami body"), the physical object — often a mirror, sword, or jewel, but sometimes a mountain or rock — in which a kami is understood to be enshrined; it is typically kept hidden from view even from priests
- ritual boundary marking generally, separating the sacred from the everyday rather than treating landscape as neutral background
- periodic renewal: the Ise Grand Shrine is rebuilt on an adjacent site every 20 years (*shikinen sengu*, a practice dated to the late 7th century and continued, with some historical interruptions, to the present), a striking case where physical impermanence and continuous renewal — not preservation of a single ancient structure — is understood as maintaining sacred continuity

## Historical development
### Early formation
Myth, court ritual, local cults, and sacred place traditions develop long before modern systematization, out of a plurality of regional and clan-based kami cults rather than one original unified religion. There is real scholarly debate — associated especially with John Breen and Mark Teeuwen's *A New History of Shinto* (2010) — over how early it is accurate to speak of "Shinto" as a single, bounded tradition at all, as opposed to a retrospective label applied to what were long a diverse, only loosely connected set of shrine cults, myths, and court rites. This is a genuinely contested historiographical position, not a fringe claim: it does not deny the antiquity of individual kami cults, shrines, or myths, but it does challenge the idea that "Shinto" names one continuous institution stretching back to prehistory in the way the word now suggests.

### Buddhist interaction
For roughly a thousand years, from Buddhism's introduction to Japan (traditionally dated to the mid-6th century) through 1868, Shinto and Buddhism are intertwined rather than totally separate institutions. This is crucial: Japan's premodern religious history is one of layering and negotiated coexistence, not two neatly bounded religions running in parallel.

The dominant theological framework for this coexistence was **honji suijaku** ("original ground, manifest trace"): the theory, elaborated especially from the Heian period (particularly within Tendai and Shingon Buddhist thought) and systematized by the Kamakura period, that Japanese kami are local manifestations or "traces" of universal Buddhas and bodhisattvas — Amaterasu, for instance, was widely identified with the cosmic Buddha Dainichi (Mahavairocana). Combinatory shrine-temple complexes (*jingu-ji*, shrine-temples) were common, with Buddhist priests performing rites at shrines and kami images housed alongside Buddhist icons — see [[../Cross-Regional/Syncretism Overview|Syncretism Overview]] for the wider comparative pattern.

### Kokugaku and the ideological groundwork
From the 17th–18th centuries, the **kokugaku** ("national learning") movement — scholars including Motoori Norinaga and Hirata Atsutane — turned to the *Kojiki* and other early texts to recover what they argued was an authentically native Japanese spirituality, uncontaminated by imported Chinese Confucian and Buddhist frameworks. Kokugaku's nativist, anti-Buddhist, anti-Chinese intellectual current supplied much of the ideological groundwork that the Meiji state would later draw on.

### Meiji restructuring and shinbutsu bunri (from 1868)
From **1868** onward, the new Meiji government reorganized shrine life through a decisive break with the honji suijaku synthesis. The **Shinbutsu Hanzenrei** (Kami and Buddhas Separation Order, issued March 1868) mandated **shinbutsu bunri**, the forcible institutional and doctrinal separation of Shinto shrines from Buddhist temples: combinatory shrine-temple complexes were dismantled, Buddhist clergy were removed from shrines, and Buddhist icons and terminology were purged from shrine precincts. In places this triggered a further, unofficial wave of violent anti-Buddhist destruction (*haibutsu kishaku*) as local officials and activists went beyond the government's own directive.

Out of this separation the state built what scholars now generally call **State Shinto** (*kokka Shinto*): a system in which shrine Shinto was administratively centralized, funded, and promoted by the government, and shrine ritual — reverence for the emperor as a divine descendant of Amaterasu, and participation in shrine rites — was officially classified as a supra-religious civic duty rather than a "religion" in the sense covered by the Meiji constitution's freedom-of-religion clause. This classification was not incidental; it let the state require shrine participation from subjects of all religious affiliations, including Buddhists and Christians, without technically breaching its own guarantee of religious freedom — a move historian Helen Hardacre and others treat as a paradigmatic case of a modern state constructing and instrumentalizing what it presented as immemorial tradition. How much of pre-Meiji shrine practice State Shinto authentically continued, versus reshaped or invented outright (standardized rites, a national shrine ranking system, mandatory shrine attendance, the ideological fusion of shrine worship with emperor-centered nationalism), is itself part of what Breen, Teeuwen, and Hardacre's scholarship investigates rather than assumes.

Modern scholarship also distinguishes several coexisting strands under the broader "Shinto" label from this period on: **Jinja Shinto** (Shrine Shinto, the mainstream shrine institution), **Kyoha Shinto** (Sect Shinto, thirteen officially recognized new religious movements with named founders and distinct doctrines, legally separated from shrine administration in the 1890s), and **Minzoku Shinto** (Folk Shinto, uncodified local and household practice, divination, and agricultural rites) — alongside State Shinto as the wartime administrative superstructure over Jinja Shinto specifically.

### Post-1945
Under Allied occupation, the **Shinto Directive** (SCAPIN-448, issued by General Headquarters on December 15, 1945, and principally drafted by William Kenneth Bunce) formally abolished State Shinto: it ended government sponsorship, funding, and promotion of shrine Shinto, dissolved the government's Shrine Bureau, and mandated separation of religion from the state, partly on the Allied assessment that State Shinto had substantially contributed to wartime nationalism and militarism. The 1946 "Humanity Declaration" (Ningen-sengen), issued by Emperor Hirohito, is often read alongside this as renouncing the emperor's claim to divine status, though scholars differ on how sharply the declaration itself actually breaks with earlier Shinto theology versus reframing it for a new political context.

Since 1945, most shrines are organized under the **Association of Shinto Shrines** (Jinja Honcho), a private religious corporation rather than a government body. Shinto remains socially and culturally central — through matsuri, life-cycle rites (shrine visits for newborns, the *shichi-go-san* children's festival, wedding rites, New Year's shrine visits), and continued reverence for the imperial institution — while questions about the political use of shrine symbolism persist: official visits by government ministers to Yasukuni Shrine, which enshrines Japan's war dead including convicted Class-A war criminals from World War II, remain a recurring source of domestic and regional (especially Chinese and Korean) political controversy, illustrating that the entanglement of Shinto with state and national identity did not end in 1945 so much as change form.

## Why it matters comparatively
Shinto is one of the clearest examples of:
- purity-conscious religion
- shrine-centered practice
- place-based sacred order
- a modern state's active restructuring, centralization, and partial invention of tradition under the banner of restoring an ancient one — a case worth comparing with other traditions in this library where similar dynamics occur, rather than treating as uniquely Japanese
- coexistence with other religions (centuries of Buddhist syncretism) without simple replacement, followed by a forced institutional divorce

## Good working summary
Shinto is a place-centered, purity-structured, shrine-based religious world built around kami, communal observance, and sacred landscape, with roots older than any unified national institution by that name. Its history only makes sense if you take both the depth of local ritual life and the specific, datable ways the modern Japanese state separated it from Buddhism, centralized it, and mobilized it politically seriously — without collapsing either half into the other.
