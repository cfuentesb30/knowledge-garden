# Buddhism Overview

## Scope
Buddhism is a family of religious, philosophical, monastic, meditative, devotional, and ritual traditions rooted in the teachings associated with Siddhartha Gautama, later remembered as the Buddha.

## Why it matters
Buddhism matters because it is one of the world's most influential religious civilizations and because it cannot be reduced to one thing.
It includes:
- monastic orders
- philosophical analysis
- ritual and devotional life
- meditation traditions
- cosmology, merit, karma, and rebirth
- lay religion as well as renunciant discipline

## Historical origin
- originates in ancient India (in what is now the Nepal/India border region), traditionally dated to the 5th century BCE
- emerges from and in conversation with the wider *sramana* movement — itinerant renunciant traditions that also produced [[Jainism]] — reacting against aspects of Brahmanical/Vedic ritual religion while still operating within the shared conceptual world of karma, rebirth, and liberation that also shapes emerging Hindu traditions (see [[Hindu Traditions Overview]])
- spreads across South Asia, Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, Central Asia, Tibet, China, Korea, Japan, and beyond

### The dating problem
Even the Buddha's dates are not settled fact. The traditional Theravada "long chronology," used across much of Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, places his death (*parinirvana*) around 544/543 BCE, yielding a birth date around 623 BCE. A widely used "corrected long chronology," reconciling Buddhist king-lists with better-attested dates for the Mauryan emperor Ashoka, instead places his death around 483/480 BCE — the "c. 563–483 BCE" dates still commonly printed in textbooks. However, a major international collaborative review of the evidence led by Heinz Bechert (*The Dating of the Historical Buddha*, 1991–1997) concluded that the traditional long chronology cannot be sustained on the evidence, and that a "short chronology" placing the Buddha's death closer to 400 BCE, with his active teaching life in the 5th–4th century BCE, is more defensible — though no single revised date commands full consensus, and some scholars have pushed back on Bechert's own reasoning in turn. What is not contested: no source contemporary with the Buddha survives: everything we have was composed, compiled, and transmitted orally for generations before being written down, which is a live methodological problem for reconstructing any specific fact about his life, not just his exact dates.

## Foundational problem
Buddhism begins from the reality of suffering, impermanence, attachment, and the unstable nature of conditioned existence.
Its goal is not merely correct belief but transformation leading toward liberation.

## Foundational frameworks
### Four Noble Truths
A core formulation, traditionally presented as the content of the Buddha's first sermon at Sarnath, concerning:
- **dukkha** — suffering, unsatisfactoriness, or the pervasive unreliability of conditioned experience (broader than "suffering" in the ordinary English sense: it includes subtle dissatisfaction even in pleasant states, since anything conditioned is impermanent)
- **samudaya** — its origin: craving (*tanha*) rooted in ignorance of how things actually arise and pass away
- **nirodha** — the possibility of cessation: suffering can end because its cause can end
- **magga** — the path leading to cessation, specified as the Eightfold Path below

### Noble Eightfold Path
An integrated path involving ethics, mental discipline, and wisdom rather than one isolated practice, traditionally grouped into three trainings: **wisdom** (right view, right intention), **ethical conduct** (right speech, right action, right livelihood), and **mental discipline** (right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration). The path is presented as gradual cultivation across all three areas together, not a sequence of separable steps or a single meditation technique.

### Karma and rebirth
Actions shape future consequences, and existence is conditioned across lives.
This creates a specific philosophical problem Buddhism has to solve that most other rebirth-affirming traditions do not face in the same form: if there is no fixed, unchanging self (*anatta* — see below), what is it that is reborn? Buddhist traditions answer this differently rather than with one settled formula: classical Theravada Abhidhamma analysis describes a causally connected but non-identical stream of mental events (sometimes illustrated with the analogy of one candle flame lighting another — connected, but not the same flame), while later Yogacara philosophy in Mahayana developed the concept of the *alaya-vijnana* ("storehouse consciousness") as a subtler continuity-bearing structure. Different traditions explain this with real philosophical divergence, not merely different metaphors for one settled account, but the basic structure — conditioned continuity without a permanent self — matters across nearly all of them.

## Councils, schism, and canon formation
Buddhist tradition holds that shortly after the Buddha's death his teachings were recited and collectively affirmed at a **First Council** at Rajagaha, and that a **Second Council** at Vaishali (traditionally placed roughly a century later) addressed a dispute over monastic discipline (*vinaya*) that led to the earliest major split in the monastic community, between the **Sthavira** ("Elders") and **Mahasamghika** ("Great Assembly") factions. Between the second and a later **Third Council** — which Theravada tradition places under the Mauryan emperor **Ashoka** (r. c. 268–232 BCE) at Pataliputra, a council whose historicity outside the Theravada textual tradition is debated — these lineages fragmented further into what later sources loosely describe as some eighteen or twenty early schools (*nikaya* Buddhism, sometimes labeled "Hinayana" in older, now-avoided polemical usage from later Mahayana perspectives). The exact number and names of these schools are not consistent across surviving sources, which is itself telling about how much of this early period is reconstructed from fragmentary and sometimes conflicting later testimony rather than contemporaneous record.

Only one of these early schools' complete canon survives intact in its original language: the **Pali Canon** (*Tipitaka*, "Three Baskets") of the **Theravada** school, preserved by Sri Lankan tradition as having been brought to the island by the monk Mahinda (traditionally Ashoka's son) in the 3rd century BCE and first committed to writing at Aluvihara, Sri Lanka, around the 1st century BCE. Other early schools' canons survive only partially — in fragments, in Sanskrit, or in Chinese and Tibetan translation (the Sarvastivada school's Agamas, for instance, are substantially preserved in Chinese translation) — a real asymmetry in the source base for reconstructing early Buddhism that scholars have to work around rather than around a single lost original.

Ashoka's own reign matters beyond the council tradition: his rock and pillar edicts (the earliest surviving epigraphic evidence for Buddhism as a state-supported tradition) and missions credited with spreading Buddhism to Sri Lanka and beyond made Mauryan imperial patronage one of the decisive historical factors in Buddhism's early expansion beyond its region of origin — a case worth comparing with the role of imperial patronage in other traditions' spread (see [[../Cross-Regional/Kingship and Divine Legitimacy|Kingship and Divine Legitimacy]]).

## Major branches
### Theravada
Strongly associated with Sri Lanka and much of Southeast Asia (Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia).
Emphasizes monastic discipline, canonical study grounded in the Pali Canon, and the arhat ideal (the practitioner who attains liberation by following the path taught by the Buddha), though popular and lay devotion — merit-making, relic and image veneration, protective ritual — remain very important in practice and are not a later corruption of an originally "pure" philosophical Theravada, a framing scholars increasingly reject as itself a modern (often Western-Protestant-influenced) projection onto the tradition.

### Mahayana
A vast family of traditions emphasizing the bodhisattva ideal — postponing one's own final liberation to work for the liberation of all beings — expanded textual canons (including sutras, such as the Prajnaparamita ["Perfection of Wisdom"] literature, the Lotus Sutra, and the Pure Land sutras, that Theravada does not recognize as canonical), and multiple philosophical schools (Madhyamaka, associated with Nagarjuna's analysis of emptiness/*sunyata*; Yogacara, associated with Asanga and Vasubandhu). Especially influential in East Asia (China, Korea, Japan, Vietnam) as well as parts of Central and South Asia historically.

The origins of Mahayana are themselves a live scholarly question rather than settled history: an older model treated it as arising from lay dissatisfaction with a supposedly narrow monastic Theravada; more recent scholarship (notably Gregory Schopen's work on inscriptional and archaeological evidence) has complicated this, suggesting Mahayana emerged gradually from within existing monastic communities, initially as a marginal minority current among various early schools rather than a unified breakaway lay movement, only becoming institutionally and numerically dominant across much of Asia considerably later than its earliest textual traces (which appear from roughly the 1st century BCE–1st century CE onward).

Within Mahayana, **Pure Land Buddhism** deserves naming in its own right rather than folding into "Mahayana" generically: centered on devotion to the Buddha **Amitabha** (Amida in Japanese) and rebirth in his Pure Land (Sukhavati) through faith and the recitation of his name (*nianfo* in Chinese, *nembutsu* in Japanese — "Namu Amida Butsu"), it became one of the most widely practiced forms of Buddhism across East Asia, particularly influential in Japan through figures like Honen and Shinran (founder of Jodo Shinshu, "True Pure Land" school), and represents one of Buddhism's major devotional/faith-oriented currents alongside its meditative and philosophical ones.

For Chan/Zen specifically, see [[Zen and Chan Buddhism]].

### Vajrayana
Strong in Tibetan and Himalayan worlds, integrating tantric methods, ritual complexity, visualization, mantra, initiation, and advanced symbolic systems within a Mahayana doctrinal base. See [[Tibetan Buddhism and Vajrayana]] for the fuller treatment — this Overview only summarizes the branch to keep the three-vehicle structure legible.

## Core themes worth studying
- **dependent origination** (*pratityasamutpada*) — the principle that all phenomena arise in dependence on conditions rather than existing independently; treated across traditions as the deepest structural insight underlying the Four Noble Truths
- **no-self** (*anatta*/*anatman*) — the analysis, found across nearly all Buddhist schools despite real philosophical variation in how it's argued, that no fixed, independent, permanent self can be found in experience; a genuinely distinctive claim relative to the traditions Buddhism emerged alongside, and one traditional sources present as a middle position between eternalism (a permanent soul) and annihilationism (nothing continues at all) rather than either extreme
- meditation and concentration (*samatha*, calming/concentration practices, and *vipassana*/*vipashyana*, insight practices) — see [[../Cross-Regional/Mysticism and Contemplative Practice|Mysticism and Contemplative Practice]]
- compassion (*karuna*) and wisdom (*prajna*) as the two poles Mahayana in particular treats as needing joint cultivation
- merit (*punya*) and devotional practice, structuring much of lay religious life
- relics, sacred images, pilgrimage, and monastic institutions
- philosophical diversity rather than one Buddhist doctrine block — Theravada Abhidhamma, Madhyamaka, Yogacara, and Vajrayana tantric philosophy disagree with each other on real points, not merely in emphasis

## Institutions and practice
Buddhism is not only meditation.
It includes:
- monasteries, historically the primary institutional setting for both textual transmission and ritual life
- ritual specialists and ordained clergy
- chanting and recitation
- pilgrimage, especially to the four traditional sites associated with the Buddha's life: **Lumbini** (traditional birthplace, in present-day Nepal), **Bodh Gaya** (site of his awakening), **Sarnath** (site of his first teaching), and **Kushinagar** (site of his death/parinirvana) — see [[../Cross-Regional/Sacred Geography and Pilgrimage|Sacred Geography and Pilgrimage]]
- image veneration in many settings
- relic devotion, including the stupa (reliquary mound) as one of Buddhism's oldest and most widespread architectural-ritual forms
- festivals, such as Vesak (commemorating the Buddha's birth, awakening, and death, observed together on a single day in much of the Theravada world but as separate observances in parts of the Mahayana world)
- merit-making and lay-monastic exchange — the reciprocal relationship in which laypeople materially support monastics (food, robes, monastery upkeep) and monastics provide ritual services, teaching, and a "field of merit" for lay giving; this exchange, not private meditation alone, is how most Buddhist societies have historically organized religious life day to day

## Why Buddhism varies so much
As Buddhism moves into new regions, it absorbs local cosmologies, political structures, devotional cultures, and ritual needs — a general pattern in religious transmission worth reading alongside [[../Cross-Regional/Syncretism Overview|Syncretism Overview]] and [[../Cross-Regional/Conversion and Mission|Conversion and Mission]].
That is why Zen (see [[Zen and Chan Buddhism]]), Tibetan tantric ritual systems (see [[Tibetan Buddhism and Vajrayana]]), Thai forest monasticism, and Pure Land devotion can all be Buddhist while looking very different in practice, institutional form, and even underlying philosophy.

## Good working summary
Buddhism is a many-layered civilization of liberation, discipline, philosophy, ritual, devotion, and monastic life, built on a shared core (Four Noble Truths, dependent origination, no-self) that different schools interpret with real, not merely cosmetic, philosophical disagreement. The biggest mistake is to confuse one school, one modern meditation image, or one national form with the whole tradition — or to treat its early history as more settled and less textually reconstructed than it actually is.
