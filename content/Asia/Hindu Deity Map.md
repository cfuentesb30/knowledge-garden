# Hindu Deity Map

## Purpose
This note is a deity-reference shelf for major Hindu divine forms and patterns. Use it as orientation alongside [[Hindu Traditions Overview]] (the fuller civilizational survey) and [[Bhakti Tradition]] (the devotional theology and history behind how many of these deities are actually worshipped) — not as a claim that all Hindu traditions organize divinity the same way. "Hinduism" is a modern umbrella term for an enormously varied family of South Asian traditions; which deities are central, how they relate to one another, and whether they are understood as distinct beings, aspects of one reality, or something else again, differs by sect, region, text, and historical period.

## A caution on "the Hindu pantheon"
There is no single canonical hierarchy. Three broad sectarian orientations organize most (not all) devotional life:
- **Vaishnavism** — centers Vishnu and/or his avatars (especially Krishna and Rama) as supreme
- **Shaivism** — centers Shiva, in forms ranging from philosophical non-dualism to ascetic and tantric practice
- **Shaktism** — centers the Goddess (Devi) in her many forms as the supreme reality, with male gods understood as deriving their power (*shakti*) from her

A Vaishnava theologian and a Shakta theologian will each describe a different deity as ultimate reality and treat the others as manifestations or subordinates of that one — this is genuine, longstanding theological disagreement within Hindu traditions, not a case that resolves neatly from outside.

## Vishnu and the avatars
**Vishnu** is the preserving/sustaining deity in the classical Puranic triad alongside Brahma (creation) and Shiva (destruction/transformation) — though in practice, most living devotional traditions center on Vishnu or Shiva (or Devi) as supreme in their own right rather than treating this triad as a functional division of equals.

Vishnu is best known through his **avatars** (*avatara*, "descent") — divine incarnations who intervene in the world at moments of crisis to restore *dharma*. The classical list of ten (**Dashavatara**) is: **Matsya** (fish), **Kurma** (tortoise), **Varaha** (boar), **Narasimha** (man-lion), **Vamana** (dwarf), **Parashurama**, **Rama**, **Krishna**, **Buddha**, and **Kalki** (yet to come). The inclusion of the Buddha as a Vishnu avatar is itself a historically significant and theologically contested move — most likely a Puranic-era strategy (from roughly the mid-1st millennium CE) for absorbing a rival tradition's founder into the Vaishnava framework rather than a claim Buddhist tradition itself makes or accepts; some regional and sectarian lists substitute Balarama (Krishna's brother) for the Buddha.

- **Krishna** — arguably the most widely beloved avatar; central to the *Bhagavad Gita* (as divine teacher and charioteer to Arjuna) and to the *Bhagavata Purana*'s narratives of his childhood and youth in Vrindavan. Krishna devotion (see [[Bhakti Tradition]]) ranges from the playful, pastoral Krishna of Vrindavan legend to the cosmic, authoritative Krishna of the Gita — traditions do not always reconcile these registers into one consistent character.
- **Rama** — hero of the *Ramayana*; embodies *dharma*, ideal kingship, and loyalty. Rama devotion is especially dominant in North Indian bhakti (Tulsidas's *Ramcharitmanas* shaped Hindi-speaking Rama worship decisively) and carries significant contemporary political weight in India, notably around the Ayodhya site tradition holds as his birthplace.

## Shiva
**Shiva** holds together an unusually wide range of associations: ascetic renunciation and yogic mastery; the paradox of creation-through-destruction; cosmic dance (as **Nataraja**, "Lord of the Dance," one of Hindu art's most recognizable images, where his dance simultaneously destroys and renews the universe); and, in tantric and esoteric currents, union with Shakti as the ground of reality itself. Iconographically distinctive markers include the third eye (associated with both destructive power and inner vision), the crescent moon and the river Ganga depicted flowing from his hair, the trident (*trishula*), and the **lingam** — an abstract cylindrical form that is Shiva's most widespread temple representation and whose interpretation (as a fertility/generative symbol, as an aniconic representation of the formless absolute, or as both at once depending on tradition and period) has been a genuine subject of both scholarly and sectarian discussion rather than a single settled reading. **Ardhanarishvara** — a composite half-male, half-female form combining Shiva and his consort — represents a distinct theological statement about the non-duality of male and female divine principle within Shaiva theology.

## Devi — the Goddess
**Devi** ("the Goddess") appears both as a general category — the divine feminine understood as supreme reality in Shakta theology — and as a set of specific, historically distinct goddesses who came to be understood as her forms:
- **Durga** — a protective warrior goddess whose defining myth, told in the *Devi Mahatmya* (part of the *Markandeya Purana*, composed roughly 400–600 CE), has the assembled male gods, unable to defeat the buffalo-demon **Mahishasura**, pool their individual powers (*shakti*) to create Durga, who alone succeeds in killing him — a myth that explicitly stages female divine power as necessary where combined male divine power fails. This narrative is the theological basis for **Navaratri/Durga Puja**.
- **Kali** — a fierce, transformative goddess associated with time, death, and the dissolution of ego and illusion; scholar David Kinsley traces her emergence as a distinctly named, iconographically stable goddess to roughly the same 5th–6th century CE window as Durga's rise to prominence, though motifs later associated with her (untamed, destructive female power) appear earlier in figures like the Vedic goddess Nirrti. Kali devotion became especially prominent in Bengal, associated with the 18th–19th century poet-devotee traditions and later with figures like Ramakrishna.
- **Lakshmi** — prosperity, fortune, and auspiciousness; Vishnu's consort, and central to household worship, especially around Diwali.
- **Saraswati** — learning, speech, music, and the arts; one of the oldest continuously worshipped goddesses in the tradition, with roots reaching back toward Vedic river/speech goddess associations.
- **Parvati** — Shiva's consort in her benign, domestic, and maternal aspect (as opposed to Durga/Kali's fierce aspect), mother of Ganesha in most tellings.

David Kinsley's *Hindu Goddesses: Visions of the Divine Feminine in the Hindu Religious Tradition* (1986) is the standard academic survey of how these figures relate historically and theologically — and is explicit that treating them as simply interchangeable "forms of one Goddess" flattens real historical and regional differences in how each was and is independently worshipped.

## Ganesha and Hanuman
- **Ganesha** — the elephant-headed remover of obstacles (or, depending on ritual context, placer of obstacles before those who need testing), invoked at the start of undertakings, journeys, and other deities' own worship. The myth of his elephant head varies by textual source — most commonly that Parvati created him independently of Shiva, and Shiva, not recognizing him, beheaded him before restoring him with an elephant's head — a story existing in multiple, not fully reconcilable, Puranic versions.
- **Hanuman** — the monkey deity central to the *Ramayana* as Rama's devoted ally and messenger; a paradigm of *bhakti* understood as loyal service (*dasya bhava* — see [[Bhakti Tradition]]) rather than romantic devotion, and one of the most widely worshipped deities in contemporary North Indian popular religion.

## Brahma
**Brahma**, the creator, occupies an unusual position: present in classical theology as one-third of the creation-preservation-destruction triad, but almost absent from contemporary popular worship — India has only a small handful of active Brahma temples (the temple at Pushkar, Rajasthan, is the best known), a scarcity that itself has attracted various origin myths and scholarly explanations (including theories about Brahma's cultic role having been effectively absorbed into Vaishnava and Shaiva theology rather than persisting as an independent object of devotion). This gap between textual prominence and devotional practice is itself worth noting as a caution against assuming a deity's place in classical theology predicts its place in lived religion.

- Gavin Flood — *An Introduction to Hinduism* (1996)
- Diana Eck — *Darsan: Seeing the Divine Image in India* (on how deities are seen/encountered ritually, not just described)
