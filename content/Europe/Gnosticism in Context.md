# Gnosticism in Context

## Scope
This note explains what people usually mean by "Gnosticism," why the label is contested, and how these movements fit into the wider religious world of late antiquity.

## Where the word comes from
"Gnosticism" is not an ancient self-designation for a religion in the way "Judaism" or "Christianity" eventually functioned. According to historian Karen King (*What Is Gnosticism?*, Harvard University Press/Belknap Press, 2003), the English word appears to have been coined by the Cambridge Platonist Henry More in 1669, in the context of Protestant anti-Catholic polemic — More used it to characterize Catholicism itself as a form of "the old abhorred Gnosticism."

More likely drew the term from the full title of Irenaeus of Lyon's 2nd-century heresiological work, conventionally known as *Against Heresies* but titled in full *On the Detection and Overthrow of the So-Called Knowledge (gnōsis)*. So the modern category "Gnosticism" descends, at two removes, from a hostile Christian polemic against a hostile Christian polemic — a genealogy worth keeping in mind whenever the term gets used as if it named a clean, stable object.

That said, the underlying Greek term *gnōstikos* ("one who has knowledge," or "pertaining to knowledge") does appear in ancient sources as a name applied to a specific, identifiable group. Irenaeus and other heresiologists use *gnōstikoi* for a group modern scholars sometimes call "classic Gnostics" or "Sethians" — and there is scholarly discussion, following Irenaeus and later Porphyry (in his *Life of Plotinus*), about whether this was a genuine self-designation for at least part of that group.

Crucially, ancient sources including Irenaeus treat the *Gnōstikoi* and the Valentinians (see [[Valentinianism]]) as related but distinct. The modern habit of using "Gnostic" as an umbrella term covering both, plus Basilideans, Thomasine Christians, Manichaeans, and more, is a modern scholarly convention, not an ancient one.

## Important caution
"Gnosticism" is useful as a shorthand, but scholars actively disagree about whether it lumps together too many different groups and texts under one label. Two influential positions frame the current debate:
- **Michael Williams**, in *Rethinking "Gnosticism": An Argument for Dismantling a Dubious Category* (Princeton University Press, 1996), argues that the standard checklist of "Gnostic" traits — anticosmic world-rejection, hatred of the body, and a supposed split between ascetic and libertine ethical extremes — does not actually hold consistently across the texts conventionally labeled Gnostic. He proposes retiring the category in favor of a narrower, more precisely defined one: "biblical demiurgical traditions," meaning currents that share a specific move of distinguishing a lower creator god from a higher, more remote divine source, while engaging seriously with Jewish and Christian scripture.
- **Karen King** likewise argues the category as commonly used tells us more about the concerns of the scholars (and the heresiologists before them) who constructed it than about a coherent ancient movement, though she is less inclined than Williams to propose a replacement label and more interested in tracing how the category itself was built.

An earlier attempt to resolve the definitional problem, the 1966 **Messina Colloquium** ("The Origins of Gnosticism," Messina, Italy), tried to distinguish a broad phenomenon of *gnosis* (knowledge-based religious currents found across many periods and cultures) from a narrower historical "Gnosticism" (specific 2nd-century movements). The colloquium's proposed definitions did not achieve lasting consensus, and the terminological problem it tried to settle is, by most accounts, still open six decades later.

Given all this, this note (and the related notes below) use "Gnostic"/"Gnosticism" as a working scholarly shorthand for a family of late antique currents — while flagging, as the scholarship above requires, that the boundaries of that family are themselves disputed.

## Core themes often associated with Gnostic-type movements
- special or saving knowledge (*gnosis*), typically understood as self-knowledge that is simultaneously knowledge of one's true divine origin
- a sharp distinction between higher divine reality and flawed material existence
- complex cosmology and emanation schemes
- intermediary beings or aeons (see [[Demiurge and Aeons]])
- a flawed, ignorant, or hostile creator/demiurge figure in many (not all) systems
- liberation through revealed knowledge rather than through faith, sacrifice, or law-observance alone

## Historical context
These movements appear within the wider late antique world shaped by:
- Judaism, including its apocalyptic and mystical strands
- Christianity, in its still-diverse and doctrinally unsettled first centuries
- Hellenistic philosophy, especially Middle Platonism — the Neoplatonist philosopher Plotinus wrote an entire treatise, *Enneads* II.9, conventionally titled "Against the Gnostics," attacking a Gnostic group active within his own Roman philosophical circle in the 3rd century. This is significant evidence that at least some Gnostic currents were not exclusively "Christian" in any simple sense, but circulated among philosophically literate non-Christian Platonists too.
- mystery traditions and their emphasis on restricted initiation
- apocalyptic and revelatory literature, a genre Judaism and Christianity both already possessed independently of anything "Gnostic"

## Debated origins
Where these currents historically came from is itself contested, and the positions below are not mutually exclusive:
- An older history-of-religions school (associated with Wilhelm Bousset and Richard Reitzenstein in the early 20th century) proposed Iranian/Zoroastrian dualism as a major source. This view is now largely regarded as overstated by specialists, though it is not entirely abandoned in every particular.
- Some scholars (following threads in Gershom Scholem's work, discussed further in [[Jewish Kabbalah in Depth]]) emphasize roots in heterodox Jewish mysticism and apocalypticism, pointing to the deep engagement many Gnostic texts show with Genesis, Exodus, and Jewish angelology.
- Others emphasize an intra-Christian trajectory: Gnostic currents as one range of options within the genuinely unsettled theological diversity of 2nd-century Christianity, before "orthodoxy" and "heresy" had been institutionally fixed.
- Adolf von Harnack's older (late 19th/early 20th century) framing of Gnosticism as "the acute Hellenization of Christianity" — implying a foreign Greek philosophical corruption of an originally Jewish-rooted faith — is now widely critiqued by scholars such as Karen King and April DeConick as underestimating how seriously many Gnostic texts engage Jewish scripture on its own terms, even while radically reinterpreting the creator figure within it. Treating this relationship as a wholesale "rejection" of Judaism oversimplifies a much more layered engagement.

## Why it matters
Gnostic currents matter because they show how unstable and intellectually competitive the late antique religious world was. Orthodoxy did not emerge into an empty room; it was one outcome among several live theological possibilities, consolidated over time through argument, institutional power, and the exclusion of rival readings.

## Texts and evidence
Much of what we know comes from:
- heresiological critiques by later orthodox writers, chiefly Irenaeus of Lyon (*Against Heresies*, c. 180 CE), Hippolytus of Rome (*Refutation of All Heresies*), Tertullian, and Epiphanius of Salamis (*Panarion*, 4th century) — all of them hostile sources writing to refute, not to neutrally describe, the movements they discuss
- the Nag Hammadi codices, discovered in 1945, which give direct access to primary texts rather than only their opponents' paraphrases (see [[Nag Hammadi and Gnostic Texts]])
- other independently surviving Coptic manuscripts, including the Berlin Gnostic Codex (found 1896, published 1955) and Codex Tchacos (containing the *Gospel of Judas*, surfaced in the 1970s–2000s) — texts that are sometimes loosely and inaccurately described as "part of Nag Hammadi" in popular accounts, though they were found separately
- the Mandaean religious community, a still-living tradition centered historically in Iraq and Iran with its own scriptures in Mandaic (a form of Aramaic); many scholars treat Mandaeism as the only Gnostic-type religious tradition to survive into the present, though Mandaeans themselves generally do not use "Gnostic" as a self-description, and applying the etic label to a living minority religion carries the same definitional risks discussed above

## Ritual and religious life
Evidence varies by group, and this is exactly why overgeneralization is dangerous. But themes can include:
- initiation
- revealed discourse, often framed as a post-resurrection dialogue between Jesus and a disciple
- symbolic ritual, including in some Valentinian sources sacramental rites resembling baptism and a "bridal chamber" rite (attested in the *Gospel of Philip*) whose precise ritual content is debated
- ascetic tendencies in some groups, though — contrary to older generalizations — some sources report the opposite, libertine tendencies attributed (often by hostile heresiologists, and therefore treated cautiously by historians) to other groups
- alternative readings of biblical narratives, especially Genesis

## Recommended resources
- Karen King, *What Is Gnosticism?* (Harvard University Press, 2003) — on the invention and historiography of the category itself.
- Michael Williams, *Rethinking "Gnosticism"* (Princeton University Press, 1996) — the major critical challenge to the category.
- Bentley Layton, *The Gnostic Scriptures* (Doubleday, 1987) — primary text collection with an influential classificatory scheme.
- Hans Jonas, *The Gnostic Religion* (Beacon Press, 1958) — historically important, shaped mid-20th-century understanding, but superseded on many points by post-Nag Hammadi scholarship; read as intellectual history of the field as much as a current reference.
- Elaine Pagels, *The Gnostic Gospels* (Random House, 1979) — an accessible, widely read entry point; useful but should be supplemented with the more technical works above, as some of its framing (e.g., treating "Gnostic" Christianity as a fairly unified feminist-friendly alternative to institutional orthodoxy) has been qualified by later scholarship.
- Proceedings of the Messina Colloquium, *The Origins of Gnosticism*, ed. Ugo Bianchi (Brill, 1967) — the classic attempt (and classic failure) to fix a consensus definition.

## Good working summary
"Gnosticism" is best treated as a contested, modern scholarly umbrella — descended from a hostile ancient polemic — for several late antique movements centered on hidden knowledge, cosmological tension, and alternative readings of the divine, world, and salvation. Whether the umbrella should even keep its current shape is itself an open scholarly question.
