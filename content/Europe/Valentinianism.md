# Valentinianism

## Scope
Valentinianism is one of the most sophisticated and influential Christian-Gnostic currents of late antiquity, associated especially with the teacher Valentinus and the later schools that developed and diversified his ideas. See [[Gnosticism in Context]] for why "Gnostic" is itself a contested umbrella term, and note that ancient heresiologists (Irenaeus, Tertullian) generally treated the Valentinians as a distinct movement influenced by, but not identical to, the group they called the *Gnōstikoi*.

## Why it matters
If people only know Gnosticism as bizarre cosmology, they miss the most intellectually serious parts.
Valentinianism matters because it shows a high-level attempt to interpret Christian revelation through a complex symbolic and cosmological system, developed by writers who saw themselves as offering a deeper, more spiritually mature reading of the same scriptures and the same Christ that other Christians of their day also claimed.

## Historical background
Almost everything reported about Valentinus's life comes from hostile sources writing decades to centuries after him, so biographical claims should be held with real caution:
- Most modern accounts place his active teaching career roughly **c. 100–160 CE**, though the exact birth and death dates are unknown and this range should be read as approximate.
- The 4th-century heresiologist Epiphanius of Salamis (*Panarion*) — writing roughly two centuries after the fact and no friend to Valentinus — reports he was born in Phrebonis in Egypt and educated in Alexandria; this detail is late and uncorroborated by earlier sources, so treat it as a claim from a hostile tradition rather than an established fact.
- He is reported to have taught in Rome from around 136 CE onward, placing him within the same mid-2nd-century Roman Christian environment as other prominent teachers of the period.
- Tertullian (*Adversus Valentinianos*) reports that Valentinus was a serious candidate for the bishopric of Rome and, having lost that contest — by most reconstructions, to Pius I — left to found his own teaching circle. This is our only ancient source for the claim, and it comes from a polemicist with an obvious interest in portraying Valentinus's departure as ambition-driven rather than principled.
- Later Valentinian branches and schools diversified significantly after Valentinus's own lifetime (see below).

Importantly, not every ancient source is hostile: Clement of Alexandria, writing in the late 2nd/early 3rd century, quotes Valentinus's own words in several places without the same polemical framing found in Irenaeus or Tertullian, and these fragments are generally treated by scholars as some of the most reliable direct evidence for what Valentinus himself actually taught, as distinct from what later Valentinian schools developed.

## Eastern and Western branches
Scholars, following the influential study by Einar Thomassen (*The Spiritual Seed: The Church of the "Valentinians"*, Brill, 2006), generally distinguish two branches of later Valentinianism, attested in different sources:
- An **Eastern branch**, associated with the teacher Theodotus and known mainly through Clement of Alexandria's excerpts from his writings (the *Excerpta ex Theodoto*).
- A **Western (or Italian) branch**, associated with Ptolemy and Heracleon, known mainly through Irenaeus's paraphrase of Ptolemy's system in *Against Heresies* Book 1, and through Heracleon's *Commentary on the Gospel of John* — the earliest known verse-by-verse Christian commentary on a gospel, preserved only in fragments quoted (in order to be refuted) by the later theologian Origen in his own commentary on John.

Thomassen argues these branches differed on real theological points, not just style — for instance, on whether the Savior descended into the world already possessing a spiritual body (the Eastern position) or received a merely psychic (soul-level) body from the lower Sophia figure at his birth (associated with the Western position) — and that the Eastern tradition likely has some claim to historical priority. This remains a specialist debate rather than settled consensus.

## Core themes
- emanational divine fullness (the *pleroma*), unfolding through a structured sequence of aeons — see [[Demiurge and Aeons]] for how this cosmology works in detail, including the specific 30-aeon system (Ogdoad, Decad, and Duodecad) that Irenaeus attributes to Ptolemy
- rupture, deficiency, and restoration, centered on the figure of Sophia, whose passion or error introduces disorder into the divine structure
- salvation through knowledge (*gnosis*), revelation, and proper spiritual alignment — though Valentinian sources also retain baptism and other sacramental practice, complicating any simple contrast between "Gnostic knowledge-salvation" and "orthodox sacramental salvation"
- strong engagement with Christian language, scripture, and figures rather than simple rejection of them: Valentinian authors wrote commentaries on John's Gospel, cited Paul extensively, and framed their teaching as the deeper meaning of texts other Christians already accepted as authoritative

## Primary Valentinian texts
Prior to 1945, virtually everything known about Valentinian teaching came through hostile heresiological paraphrase. The Nag Hammadi discovery (see [[Nag Hammadi and Gnostic Texts]]) changed that by preserving Valentinian-associated texts in their own words, including:
- the ***Gospel of Truth*** — whose authorship is genuinely disputed. Irenaeus mentions a Valentinian "Gospel of Truth" by name, and some scholars have proposed Valentinus himself as author (or as the author of an earlier version underlying the Nag Hammadi copy); this identification is possible but not proven, and should be presented as a live scholarly question rather than settled fact.
- the ***Tripartite Tractate*** (Nag Hammadi Codex I) — a lengthy, systematic Valentinian theological treatise, notable because its account of the pleroma differs in structural detail from Irenaeus's report of Ptolemy's system (it does not organize the aeons into the same strict male-female paired syzygies), which is itself evidence of real internal diversity within what heresiologists lumped together as "the Valentinians."
- the ***Gospel of Philip*** — a collection of theological excerpts including sacramental teaching (on baptism, chrism, eucharist, and a much-discussed "bridal chamber" rite) associated with Valentinian circles.
- the ***Interpretation of Knowledge*** and ***A Valentinian Exposition***, both further systematic or liturgical Valentinian material from Nag Hammadi.

## Why it is distinctive
Valentinianism often appears more intellectually integrated and scripturally engaged than the crude caricature of "Gnosticism" found in later polemic.
That is part of why it became such a serious opponent in heresiological literature — Irenaeus devotes the largest portion of Book 1 of *Against Heresies* specifically to refuting Ptolemy's Valentinian system, more space than he gives to any other rival teaching.

## Authority and interpretation
Valentinians often competed not by denying Christianity outright but by offering deeper readings of Christian truth, scripture, and salvation.
That made the conflict partly one of interpretation and authority, not only belief labels.

## Why it matters for study
Valentinianism shows:
- that early Christianity had serious internal metaphysical competition, conducted by teachers who considered themselves fully Christian
- that cosmology and scriptural interpretation were tightly linked, and that "orthodoxy" and "heresy" were partly outcomes of interpretive and institutional contest rather than self-evident from the start
- that orthodoxy was consolidated through argument, exclusion, and institutional struggle — a process this note's sources (both Valentinian and heresiological) let us watch happening in something close to real time

## Recommended resources
- Bentley Layton, *The Gnostic Scriptures* (Doubleday, 1987) — primary Valentinian texts in translation with scholarly introduction.
- Einar Thomassen, *The Spiritual Seed: The Church of the "Valentinians"* (Brill, 2006) — the major modern monograph, source of the Eastern/Western branch analysis above.
- Karen King, *What Is Gnosticism?* (Harvard University Press, 2003).
- Elaine Pagels, *The Gnostic Gospels* (1979) — usefully accessible, with supplementation from the more technical works above.
- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies*, Book 1 (available in English translation, e.g. via Ante-Nicene Fathers) — the primary hostile source for Ptolemy's system; read critically, as a polemic rather than a neutral report.

## Good working summary
Valentinianism is one of the most important examples of Christian-Gnostic thought: sophisticated, scripturally engaged, cosmologically layered, and deeply relevant to the history of orthodoxy and heresy — and, thanks to Nag Hammadi, one of the few such currents we can now read partly in its own voice rather than only through its opponents' paraphrase.
