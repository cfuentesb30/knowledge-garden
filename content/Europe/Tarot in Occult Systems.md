# Tarot in Occult Systems

## Scope
This note focuses on how tarot shifted from a 15th-century Italian card game into an occult symbolic technology in modern esotericism — and is explicit about the roughly 350-year gap between the two.

## Why it matters
A lot of people meet tarot only as pop divination.
But in modern occultism, tarot becomes a symbolic map linking imagery, initiation, Kabbalah, correspondences, and magical interpretation — a dense operating system built on top of what began as ordinary playing cards.

## Historical caution: the game came first
This is the single most important fact for studying tarot seriously, and popular occult writing routinely obscures it.

Tarot began in mid-15th-century northern Italy as a card game — *tarocchi* (Italian) or *trionfi* ("triumphs," the origin of the English "trump"). The earliest documented references are account-book records from the Este court at Ferrara in 1442; the most famous surviving early decks are the hand-painted **Visconti-Sforza** cards made for the ducal court of Milan around the 1440s. Michael Dummett's landmark study *The Game of Tarot* (1980) and his work on the Visconti-Sforza cards established, through painstaking documentary and card-game research, that tarot's 22 trump cards (the later "Major Arcana") and four-suit, court-card structure functioned purely as a trick-taking game for roughly three centuries before anyone attached esoteric meaning to them. Dummett traced three regional Italian card-order traditions (Milan, Ferrara, Bologna), all games, none divinatory.

There is **no evidence** of tarot being used for divination or occult purposes before the late 18th century, and no evidence at all connecting it to ancient Egypt, the Kabbalah, or any lost esoteric tradition prior to that point. This is a settled matter among card historians (Dummett, Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis), even though it remains one of the most persistent claims in popular tarot writing.

## The occult invention of tarot: late 18th century France

### Antoine Court de Gébelin (1781)
The occult reinterpretation of tarot begins with a specific, datable event: in 1781, the French Protestant pastor and antiquarian **Antoine Court de Gébelin** published an essay on tarot as part of his sprawling multi-volume *Le Monde Primitif*. He claimed — with, in Dummett's assessment, essentially no historical evidence — that the tarot trumps were a disguised survival of the ancient Egyptian "Book of Thoth," secretly preserved by Romani card-readers after the destruction of Egyptian civilization. A contributor to the same volume, the **Comte de Mellet**, was the first to link the 22 trump cards to the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet, seeding the Kabbalistic association that would become central to later occult tarot.

Court de Gébelin's Egyptian claim was a product of the intense European "Egyptomania" of the era — decades before hieroglyphics could even be read (Champollion's decipherment came in 1822) — and it has no support in later Egyptological or card-historical research. It nonetheless became the founding myth of occult tarot and is still repeated uncritically in some popular sources today.

### Etteilla (Jean-Baptiste Alliette)
**Jean-Baptiste Alliette**, working under the reversed-spelling pen name **Etteilla**, took Court de Gébelin's Egyptian claim and, from 1785 onward, built the first deck specifically designed for divination and the first systematic occult tarot method — including his own reordering of the cards and his own claimed Egyptian pedigree. Etteilla was a professional diviner, and later occultists (Lévi among them) treated his commercially oriented fortune-telling approach with some disdain even while building on the territory he opened.

### Éliphas Lévi and the Kabbalistic synthesis
The French occultist **Éliphas Lévi** (Alphonse Louis Constant), writing especially in *Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie* (1854–56), is the figure most responsible for rigorously fusing the 22 trumps with the 22 Hebrew letters and the Kabbalistic Tree of Life into something resembling a coherent symbolic system, rather than Court de Gébelin's looser Egyptian speculation. See [[Levi in Context]] for Lévi's broader role as a bridge between Renaissance occult philosophy and the later revival. Lévi's tarot-Kabbalah correspondence — however historically invented — became the template nearly all subsequent occult tarot systems inherit.

## Golden Dawn systematization
The **Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn** (founded 1888; see [[Golden Dawn]]) took Lévi's fusion and built it into a dense, internally consistent correspondence system: each trump attributed to a Hebrew letter, a path on the Tree of Life, an astrological sign or planet, an element, and a place in initiatory grade training. This GD correspondence table — not the original 15th-century game, and not Lévi's looser scheme — is the direct ancestor of most tarot symbolism used by practitioners today, including decks that have no institutional connection to the Golden Dawn at all.

### The Rider-Waite-Smith deck (1909)
**Pamela Colman Smith**, a Golden Dawn initiate and professional illustrator, was commissioned by fellow Golden Dawn member **Arthur Edward Waite** to illustrate a new deck, published in 1909 by the Rider company. It was the first widely circulated deck to give the Minor Arcana pip cards (not just the trumps) full illustrative scenes rather than simple suit-and-number patterns — a major innovation for readability that helped make it the best-selling and most imitated tarot deck of the 20th and 21st centuries. Smith's own role was for decades minimized in occult and commercial credit (the deck was long called simply "the Waite deck" or "Rider deck"); it is now more often referred to as the **Rider-Waite-Smith** deck in recognition of her authorship of the imagery.

### The Thoth deck (1938–1943)
**Aleister Crowley**, another Golden Dawn-trained occultist (see [[Crowley in Context]]), collaborated with artist **Lady Frieda Harris** over roughly five years to produce the **Thoth Tarot**, first published in limited form in the 1940s. It is a denser, more explicitly Thelemic and Kabbalistic reworking of GD correspondences, integrating astrology, alchemy, and Crowley's own magical system into every card.

## Why tarot becomes powerful in occult systems
Tarot is useful to occultists because it can function simultaneously as:
- symbolic language (each card as a compressed unit of correspondence)
- meditative and "pathworking" map (especially once tied to the Tree of Life)
- divinatory tool
- initiatory diagram set
- a portable, physically shuffleable correspondence system — unlike a book, it can generate new combinations

## Major occult links
In later occult systems, tarot is connected to:
- Kabbalah / sefirotic structures (see [[Kabbalah and Christian Kabbalah]])
- astrology and elemental systems
- ceremonial magic (see [[Ceremonial Magic]])
- pathworking and symbolic meditation
- Golden Dawn-style synthesis, inherited even by decks and traditions that reject the order's other content

## Why it matters historically
Tarot becomes one of the clearest examples of how the occult revival repurposes older cultural material into a denser symbolic machine.
It is not an inherited ancient wisdom system; it is a game that was re-engineered into one, in a documented, traceable sequence: game → Egyptian-origin myth (Court de Gébelin) → divination practice (Etteilla) → Kabbalistic system (Lévi) → initiatory correspondence table (Golden Dawn) → mass-market pictorial deck (Waite/Smith) and Thelemic deck (Crowley/Harris).

## Important caution
Not every tarot tradition means the same thing.
There are popular/entertainment, psychological (post-Jungian archetypal readings), commercial-divinatory, and ceremonial-magical uses, and they rest on very different claims about what the cards actually do. Collapsing them all into one usage — or assuming any of them represents "the true ancient meaning" of the cards — leads to confusion. The cards' 15th-century game function and their 18th–20th-century occult function are historically separate phenomena joined only by shared imagery.

## Good working summary
In occult systems, tarot becomes more than cards: it becomes a symbolic operating map connecting images, correspondences, divination, and initiatory meaning. But this is a documented 18th–20th century invention layered onto a 15th-century Italian trick-taking game, built through a specific, traceable chain of individuals — Court de Gébelin, Etteilla, Lévi, the Golden Dawn, Waite and Smith, Crowley and Harris — not a survival of ancient Egyptian or Kabbalistic wisdom.
