# Cults and New Religious Movements

## Scope
This note separates the loaded word "cult" from the more useful academic category of new religious movements (NRMs), traces where both terms actually come from, and lays out the real scholarly debate over coercive control — while still taking manipulation and abuse seriously as things that do happen.

## Why it matters
The word **cult** is emotionally powerful but analytically sloppy.
People use it to mean:
- a minority or unfamiliar religion
- a weird ritual group
- a manipulative leader-centered group
- a dangerous coercive movement
- anything they personally dislike or find threatening

That range makes the word nearly useless for careful comparative analysis, and its casual use tends to import a value judgment (usually: "not a real religion, and dangerous") rather than describe anything precise.

## Where the words come from
### "Cult"
The word derives from Latin *cultus* — "worship," "care," or "cultivation" — and was originally neutral, describing devotion to any deity or saint (the "cult of Saint James" is standard, non-pejorative academic usage in religious studies even today). By the 19th century it had also acquired a pejorative sense applied to excessive devotion toward secular figures, and around 1900 this pejorative use was picked up by anthropologists describing what they characterized as "primitive" religious practice — a framing this project treats as a loaded term to avoid in its own descriptive vocabulary, for the same reason it avoids "cult" itself. By the mid-to-late 20th century, especially in American popular usage after a series of high-profile scandals and tragedies (below), "cult" had settled into its current overwhelmingly negative connotation.

### "New religious movement" (NRM)
The now-standard scholarly alternative emerged as a distinct academic subfield in the 1970s. The English term is a direct calque of the Japanese *shinshūkyō* ("new religion"), coined by Japanese sociologists to describe the wave of new religious groups that emerged in Japan after the Second World War; Western scholars, including **Eileen Barker** and **J. Gordon Melton**, adopted "new religious movement" in preference to "cult" specifically to strip out the built-in value judgment and allow the groups to be studied on comparable, less loaded terms.

## Better distinction

### New Religious Movements (NRMs)
A neutral academic term for newer religious formations, alternative spiritual groups, and emerging traditions, regardless of their theology or organizational structure. Not all NRMs are abusive, and treating "new" as inherently suspicious ignores that every long-established religion was, at some point, a new religious movement viewed with the same mixture of curiosity and alarm that greets new movements today — early Christianity inside the Roman Empire, Methodism in 18th-century England, the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in 19th-century America, and Seventh-day Adventism all attracted exactly the kind of "dangerous fringe group" rhetoric now aimed at newer movements.

### "Cult" in popular/critical use
Often refers to groups marked by:
- manipulative or unaccountable leadership
- heavy psychological control
- social isolation
- economic or sexual exploitation
- severe information control
- apocalyptic pressure or totalizing demands

These features can and do occur — the question is whether "cult" is the right analytical tool for identifying them, or whether it just labels groups its user finds unfamiliar or frightening.

## The brainwashing debate
This is the central live controversy in the academic study of coercive religious groups, and it deserves to be named directly rather than assumed settled in either direction.

**Robert Jay Lifton**, a psychiatrist, developed an influential model of "thought reform" from his 1950s interviews with American servicemen and Chinese citizens subjected to Communist Party indoctrination during and after the Korean War, identifying eight characteristic techniques (including milieu control, mystical manipulation, and the demand for purity). Anti-cult activists and some clinical psychologists — most prominently **Margaret Singer** — later applied this thought-reform model directly to religious NRMs, arguing that groups like the Unification Church, Scientology, or the Hare Krishna movement used comparable coercive techniques to recruit and retain members, often serving as expert witnesses in "deprogramming" and custody litigation on this basis.

This application has been substantially rejected within mainstream academic sociology of religion. In 1987, the American Psychological Association's own **Board of Social and Ethical Responsibility for Psychology declined to endorse a task-force report (the "DIMPAC" report) chaired by Singer**, on the grounds that its brainwashing theory "lacks the scientific rigor and evenhanded critical approach necessary for APA imprimatur"; a 1990 federal court ruling (*United States v. Fishman*) subsequently excluded Singer's brainwashing testimony as not accepted mainstream science. Scholars including **Eileen Barker** (whose fieldwork among Unification Church members found voluntary attrition rates far higher than brainwashing theory would predict) and **J. Gordon Melton** have argued, using longitudinal data, that most NRM members join and leave through recognizable, ordinary processes of social influence and personal choice rather than irresistible coercive technique — and that brainwashing theory, applied wholesale to religious conversion, is empirically weak and functions mainly to justify coercive "deprogramming" interventions against people who have made a disfavored but voluntary religious choice.

This does not mean coercive control never happens inside religious groups — it demonstrably does, in well-documented cases. It means the all-or-nothing "brainwashing" frame is not the tool most sociologists of religion currently use to establish it; see [[Cult Dynamics - Charisma, Control, and Exit Costs]] for the more structural, case-by-case diagnostic approach the field has largely moved toward instead (leadership accountability, information control, exit costs) rather than a single unfalsifiable mechanism applied across every unfamiliar group.

## "Cult" as a tool of religious persecution
The label is not only a Western popular-culture problem. Governments have used "cult" or local equivalents to delegitimize and suppress minority religious movements they find politically threatening. The clearest recent case: the Chinese government designated **Falun Gong** a *xie jiao* ("heterodox teaching," commonly if imprecisely translated "evil cult") in 1998 and outlawed it in 1999, followed by a documented campaign of mass detention, and reports of torture and organ harvesting from independent human rights investigators. International scholars of new religious movements have generally concluded Falun Gong does not exhibit the coercive-control features the "cult" label implies, and that the label functioned in this case primarily as a legal instrument for suppressing a religious movement the state considered a rival source of mass loyalty — not as an accurate sociological description.

## Why the distinction matters
Without a careful distinction, analysis collapses.
A strange or unfamiliar religion is not automatically abusive.
An abusive group is not harmless just because it presents itself, or is legally recognized, as a religion.
And "cult" can be deployed dishonestly in both directions — to demonize a harmless minority religion, or to launder real coercive abuse behind the protective label of "just an unusual faith."

## Better evaluation questions
- how centralized and unaccountable is leadership?
- how much control exists over members' relationships, information, or physical movement?
- are exit costs high — financial, social, familial, or psychological?
- is secrecy protective, initiatory, or actively coercive? (compare [[Secrecy, Prestige, and Symbolic Capital in Esoteric Orders]])
- is the group parasitic on fear, dependency, or exploitation, independent of what its doctrines claim?

## Historical examples and caution
Some of the most consequential documented cases of coercive religious control ending in mass harm — the **Peoples Temple** mass deaths at Jonestown, Guyana (1978); the **Branch Davidian** standoff and fire at Waco (1993); **Heaven's Gate**'s mass suicide (1997); **Aum Shinrikyo**'s 1995 sarin gas attack on the Tokyo subway — are genuinely severe and genuinely instructive, and it would be a mistake to let a defense of the term "new religious movement" soften how seriously scholars take them. But these cases are also atypical, heavily publicized outliers, not representative of the very large number of NRMs that never approach this level of harm. Treating every unfamiliar new religion as a Jonestown-in-waiting is exactly the kind of aesthetics-substituting-for-analysis this note warns against; so is refusing to take documented coercion seriously once it is actually present.

## Recommended resources
- Eileen Barker — *The Making of a Moonie: Choice or Brainwashing?* (1984), and her founding of INFORM (1988), a UK charity providing evidence-based information on NRMs
- J. Gordon Melton — *Encyclopedia of American Religions*, and "Another Look at New Religions" (1993)
- Janja Lalich — on bounded choice and cultic dynamics from a perspective more sympathetic to coercion claims
- Robert Jay Lifton — *Thought Reform and the Psychology of Totalism* (1961), the original (non-religious) source of the eight-criteria model
- Benjamin Zablocki — for the strongest recent scholarly defense of a qualified brainwashing model, representing the minority position in the current academic debate

## Good working summary
"Cult" is usually a warning word, not a precise category, and its history runs from neutral Latin "worship" through 19th-century pejorative drift to a politically loaded 20th-century label that has been used both to name real coercive abuse and to persecute harmless or merely unfamiliar minority religions. For serious study, separate the neutral category of new religious movement from the contested and specific claim of coercive control, and judge structure, accountability, and documented harm directly rather than reaching for the word "cult" as a substitute for analysis.
